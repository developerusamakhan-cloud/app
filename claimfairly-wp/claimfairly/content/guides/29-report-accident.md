After a crash, there is more than one clock running. Your insurance policy expects to hear about the accident quickly. Your state may require a police or DMV report within days. And there is a much longer deadline for filing a lawsuit if the claim cannot be settled. Mixing them up is one of the easiest ways to weaken a good claim.

This guide answers the question **how long do I have to report an accident**, deadline by deadline, and explains what happens if you report late. It is general information for US drivers. Your policy and your state's rules are what actually apply, so check both.

## The short answer

| Who you report to | Typical deadline | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Your own insurer | "Promptly" or "as soon as practicable," often meaning within days | Your policy |
| Police | Right away if anyone is hurt; rules vary for property-only crashes | State law |
| State DMV or similar agency | Often within days when there is an injury or damage above a set amount | State law |
| The other driver's insurer | As soon as you decide to claim against them | Practical, not a fixed rule |
| A lawsuit, if the claim does not settle | Usually one to several years | Your state's statute of limitations |

## Reporting to your own insurer

Almost every auto policy requires you to notify your insurer of an accident **promptly**, even if you were not at fault and even if you plan to claim against the other driver. Policies usually do not give an exact number of days. They use words like "promptly" or "as soon as practicable." In practice, that means calling or using the insurer's app within a day or two whenever you can.

Some coverages have stricter rules. For example, some policies require a hit-and-run to be reported to the police within a short time, sometimes 24 hours, for uninsured motorist coverage to apply. Read the "Duties after an accident or loss" section of your policy so you know the exact wording.

Reporting an accident is not the same as filing a claim on your policy. You can report it, explain that you will claim against the other driver, and keep your options open.

## Reporting to the police

Call 911 right away if anyone is hurt, if a driver seems impaired, or if the crash blocks traffic. For property-only crashes, rules vary: some states require a police report above a certain damage amount, and some police departments do not respond to minor crashes at all. Even when it is optional, a police report is useful evidence about fault, and insurers often ask for it.

## Reporting to your state

Many states require drivers to file their own written report with the DMV or a similar agency when a crash causes injury, death, or damage above a set dollar amount, sometimes within as little as 10 days. California's SR-1 form is a well known example. These are separate from the police report and from your insurance claim. Check your state DMV's website for the rule and the form.

## What happens if you report late

A late report does not automatically end your claim, but it can cause real problems:

- **Your own insurer may deny coverage** for breaching the notice requirement. In many states the insurer must show that the delay actually harmed its investigation, but you do not want to be arguing that point.
- **Evidence disappears.** Damage gets repaired, witnesses forget, and video from nearby cameras is overwritten.
- **Your credibility suffers.** An adjuster may wonder why you waited, especially with injury claims.
- **State penalties** can apply for missing a required DMV report, in some states including license consequences.

If you have already waited, report now, explain the reason briefly and honestly, and send what evidence you have.

## The deadline that matters most: the statute of limitations

Every state sets a time limit for filing a lawsuit over a car accident. For injury claims it is commonly two or three years, and some states are shorter or longer. Property damage claims can have a different limit. Claims against a government vehicle often require a formal notice within a much shorter time, sometimes a few months.

Negotiating with an insurer does **not** stop this clock. If your claim is not settled and the deadline is getting close, talk to a lawyer well before it runs out. Our [state rules hub](/states/) summarizes each state's fault system and filing deadline.

## A simple timeline to follow

1. **At the scene:** call 911 if needed, take photos, exchange information.
2. **Same day or next day:** report to your insurer and get a claim number.
3. **Within the first week:** file any required state report, get the police report number, and see a doctor if you have any symptoms.
4. **While you treat:** keep records and follow your doctor's plan. Our guide on [what to do after a car accident](/what-to-do-after-a-car-accident/) has the full checklist.
5. **Well before the statute of limitations:** settle, or get legal advice about filing.
