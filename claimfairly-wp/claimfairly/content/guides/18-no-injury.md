When nobody is hurt in a crash, most people think of the claim as "getting the car fixed." The repair is the biggest piece, but it is rarely the only one. A property-only claim against the at-fault driver can include a rental car, towing, your belongings, the time your car was out of service and, the one most people miss, the resale value your car lost.

This guide covers what a **car accident settlement with no injury** can include, how to document each item, and when it still makes sense to get checked by a doctor.

## First, are you sure you're not hurt?

Some injuries take time to show. Whiplash, back strains and concussions often appear a day or two after a crash, once adrenaline wears off. If you notice neck pain, headaches, dizziness or stiffness, see a doctor and tell the insurer the injury part of the claim is still open. Settling a "no injury" claim with a broad release can end your right to claim injuries later, so read any release carefully before you sign. Our pages on [whiplash](/injuries/whiplash-car-accident-settlement/) and [concussions](/injuries/concussion-car-accident-settlement/) describe the symptoms to watch for.

## 8 things you can claim without an injury

### 1. Repairs

The cost to restore your car to its condition before the crash, using a shop you choose in most states. If the shop finds hidden damage, it sends a supplement for the extra work.

### 2. The car's value if it is totaled

If repair would cost too much compared with the car's value, the insurer pays its actual cash value instead. Our guides on [what happens when your car is totaled](/what-happens-when-your-car-is-totaled/) and [how insurers value a totaled car](/total-loss-car-value/) explain how to check that number.

### 3. Rental car or loss of use

The at-fault driver's insurer generally owes you a comparable rental for a reasonable repair period. If you did not rent one, some states allow compensation for the loss of use of your car anyway.

### 4. Towing and storage

Keep the receipts. Storage fees add up quickly, so move the car to the repair shop or your home as soon as you can.

### 5. Diminished value

Even after a perfect repair, a car with a crash on its history report sells for less. In most states, you can claim that lost value from the at-fault driver's insurer. Insurers often use the "17c" formula, which tends to produce a low number. Our [diminished value calculator](/diminished-value-calculator/) shows what that formula gives, and our [diminished value guide](/what-is-diminished-value/) explains how to claim more with an independent appraisal.

### 6. Personal property

Items damaged in the crash, like a phone, laptop, glasses or a child car seat. Bring receipts or proof of value. Many safety experts recommend replacing a child seat after a moderate or severe crash, so ask the insurer to cover it.

### 7. Out-of-pocket costs

Rideshare trips while you had no car, a deductible you paid under your own policy, and fees for a police report all count if you can document them.

### 8. Time off work for the claim

Some insurers will consider wages you lost while dealing with inspections or repairs, though this is less consistently paid. If you ask, include proof from your employer.

## A worked example

A driver runs a stop sign and hits Maria's 3-year-old SUV, worth $30,000 before the crash, with 25,000 miles. Nobody is hurt.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Repairs, including a supplement | $7,800 |
| Rental, 12 days | $540 |
| Towing and two days of storage | $310 |
| Child car seat replacement | $220 |
| Diminished value (17c formula, moderate damage) | $1,200 |
| **Total claim** | **$10,070** |

The diminished value line uses the insurer formula: $30,000 × 10% × 0.50 for moderate damage × 0.80 for 20,000 to 39,999 miles = $1,200. An independent appraisal might find a larger loss, and Maria could ask for that instead with the report attached.

Without asking, Maria might have received only the $7,800 repair payment. The other $2,270 is money many people never claim.

## Is it worth hiring a lawyer for a property-only claim?

Usually not. Most personal injury lawyers work on contingency and take on few property-only cases, because the amounts are smaller. You can handle these claims yourself in most cases. If the insurer refuses to pay a reasonable amount, small claims court is a realistic option for many property disputes, and it does not require a lawyer.

## How to present the claim

1. Open a claim with the at-fault driver's insurer and get the claim number.
2. Collect every receipt and estimate in one folder.
3. Send a short written demand listing each item and the total. Our [demand letter generator](/demand-letter-generator/) has a property damage version.
4. Ask for a written response by a specific date.
5. Keep your own insurer informed, in case you need to claim under your collision coverage instead.

If you have not been through a claim before, our [claim process guide](/car-insurance-claim-process/) explains every step from the first call to the final check.

## Mistakes that shrink a property claim

**Settling the repair before the shop finishes.** Hidden damage often shows up once the car is taken apart. If you accept a final payment too early, getting the supplement paid can be harder. Let the shop send supplements directly to the insurer.

**Not asking for the rental.** Many people pay for rideshares or borrow a car without asking. If the other driver was at fault, ask for a rental in writing on day one.

**Forgetting diminished value.** Insurers rarely offer it unless you ask. The window to claim it closes when you sign a release that covers property damage.

**Throwing away receipts.** Towing, storage, rideshares and replacement items are only paid with proof. Photograph every receipt the day you get it.

**Signing a broad release.** Some property releases are worded to cover injuries too. If you have any chance of an injury claim, ask for a property damage only release.

## When your own insurer pays

If you use your own collision coverage, you pay the deductible, and your insurer may then pursue the at-fault driver's insurer through subrogation. If it succeeds, you may get your deductible back. Diminished value is generally not covered under your own collision coverage in most states, so it is usually claimed against the at-fault driver.
