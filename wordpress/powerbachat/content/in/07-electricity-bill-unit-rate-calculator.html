The words "unit rate" cause a lot of confusion. Most bills do not have a single unit rate. They have several, one per slab, plus charges that are not per unit at all. Understanding that is the key to understanding your bill.

What a unit is

A unit is one kilowatt-hour: the energy a 1,000 watt appliance uses in one hour. A 100 watt fan running for ten hours uses one unit. A 1.5 ton AC on a hot night uses roughly one unit an hour. Your meter counts these units, and the bill multiplies them by the rates for your state.

How to calculate a bill from unit rates

  1. Find your units for the billing period on the bill or by subtracting meter readings.
  2. Split them into slabs. For example, with slabs of 0 to 100, 101 to 300 and above 300, a 380 unit bill has 100 units in the first slab, 200 in the second and 80 in the third.
  3. Multiply each slab by its rate and add them up. This is the energy charge.
  4. Add the fixed charge. It is usually per kilowatt of sanctioned load per month.
  5. Add duty or tax. Usually a percentage of the energy charge, sometimes of the fixed charge too.
  6. Add any adjustment printed on the bill, such as a fuel or power purchase adjustment.

Telescopic and non-telescopic slabs

Most states use telescopic slabs, where each block of units is charged at its own rate. Going a few units into a higher slab only makes those few units dearer. Some states switch to non-telescopic billing above a certain level, where every unit is charged at the rate of the slab your total falls in. Kerala does this above 250 units a month, which is why a KSEB bill can jump sharply at that line. Our KSEB bill calculator shows the effect clearly.

Your real cost per unit

The most useful single number is your own effective rate: the total bill divided by the units. It includes every slab, fixed charge, tax and adjustment. A small home with a high fixed charge can have a surprisingly high effective rate, while a large home in a state with steep slabs will have a high rate for a different reason. Knowing your effective rate tells you what a saving is really worth. If your rate is ₹12 a unit, a BLDC fan that saves 15 units a month saves ₹180 a month, not the ₹75 you might guess from the first slab.

Using unit rates to compare appliances

Once you know your top rate, you can compare appliances properly. An inverter AC that uses 300 fewer units a year than an old one saves 300 times your top rate, every year. A solar water heater that removes a geyser's 100 units a month in winter saves that amount at your top rate too. Our guide on how an electricity bill is calculated goes deeper into which units are worth saving first.

A worked example by hand

Suppose your bill shows 380 units, with slabs of 0 to 100 at one rate, 101 to 300 at a higher rate and above 300 at a higher rate still, plus a fixed charge and 10 percent duty. You would calculate:

  1. First 100 units times the first rate.
  2. Next 200 units times the second rate.
  3. Last 80 units times the third rate.
  4. Add the three for the energy charge, then add the fixed charge.
  5. Add 10 percent of the energy charge as duty.

Compare your answer with the energy charge, fixed charge and duty lines on the printed bill. If they match, the only difference left should be the adjustment line and any arrears. If they do not match, check the units and the slab limits for your category, since some states use different slabs for rural and urban homes.

Monthly and two-month bills

Some boards bill monthly and others every two months. Where billing is bi-monthly, the slab limits may be set for the full two months, as in Tamil Nadu, or set monthly and doubled, as in Kerala. Always check which applies before comparing your units with a slab table, or your calculation will be off by a whole slab.

Prepaid meters and unit rates

Several states are moving homes to smart prepaid meters. The unit rates do not change, but the way you pay does: you recharge in advance and the balance falls as you use power. Fixed charges are usually deducted daily. If you are on a prepaid meter, divide a month's recharges by a month's units to find your effective rate, and use the calculator to plan how much to recharge.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate my electricity bill from the unit rate?

Split your units into your state's slabs, multiply each by its rate, add the fixed charge and then the duty or tax. The electricity bill unit rate calculator above does this for the boards we cover.

Why is my effective unit rate higher than the slab rate?

Because fixed charges, duty and adjustments are added on top, and because heavier use puts more units in higher slabs.

What if my state is not in the list?

Use the step-by-step method above with the rates printed on your own bill. We add states as we verify their tariffs.

Is a lower unit rate always better?

Not on its own. A low slab rate with a high fixed charge can still make a small bill expensive. Compare total bills at your own usage, or your effective rate, rather than any single number.
