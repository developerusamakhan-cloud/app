When load-shedding hits in the middle of a hot night, a working backup makes all the difference. Buying a UPS for home sounds simple, but shop labels mix up VA and watts, batteries vary a lot, and the wrong choice either trips every evening or eats batteries in a year. This guide explains how to size one properly, how long backup actually lasts, what it adds to your bill and when solar is the better route.

Decide what you want to run

Before comparing any UPS for home use, list the appliances you want on backup. For most families that means a few fans, some LED lights, the Wi-Fi router and phone chargers. Perhaps a TV. Rough figures:

  • Ceiling fan: around 70 to 100 watts for an older fan, 30 to 40 watts for a DC or energy saver fan.
  • LED bulb: around 10 to 15 watts.
  • Wi-Fi router: around 10 watts.
  • LED TV: around 60 to 120 watts depending on size.
  • Laptop charger: around 50 to 70 watts.

Three ceiling fans, six LED bulbs, a router and a TV add up to roughly 400 to 500 watts. That is a typical household backup load. An AC, iron, water pump or electric kettle is a different story. Those need far more power, and a normal home UPS is not designed for them.

VA vs watts

UPS units are sold by their VA rating, such as 1000 VA or 1500 VA. That is not the same as watts. To convert, multiply the VA by the power factor of the unit, which the manufacturer lists in the specifications. Many home units have a power factor somewhere between 0.6 and 0.8, and some newer ones are higher.

So a 1000 VA unit with a power factor of 0.7 can supply about 700 watts. For a 450 watt load, that leaves a healthy margin. Aim to load a UPS to no more than about 70 to 80 percent of its watt rating. A unit running flat out every night runs hot and wears out sooner.

How many batteries

Smaller units usually run on one 12 volt battery. Larger ones use two batteries at 24 volts, and bigger inverters may use four. The battery count is fixed by the unit's design, so check it before you buy.

What you choose is the battery size, measured in amp hours (Ah). A bigger battery gives longer backup at the same load. In Pakistan, the common choices are a tubular battery, a cheaper flat plate battery, or lithium. A tubular battery handles daily deep discharge better than a flat plate one and usually lasts longer. Lithium costs more upfront but lasts more cycles and charges faster. Our comparison of lithium vs tubular batteries goes into the trade off in detail.

Why pure sine wave matters

The cheapest units produce a modified or square wave output. That is rougher than grid power. Fans hum louder and run warmer, some chargers and LED drivers fail early, and sensitive electronics may not like it.

A pure sine wave unit produces power that is close to what comes from the grid. Fans run quietly, and laptops, TVs and inverter fridges are much happier. The price gap is smaller than it used to be, and for most homes a pure sine wave unit is worth it.

How long backup lasts

You can estimate backup time with simple arithmetic:

  1. Multiply battery voltage by amp hours to get watt hours. One 12 volt, 150 Ah battery holds about 1,800 watt hours.
  2. Take only the usable part. For lead acid batteries, using about half the capacity each time helps them last longer, so call it roughly 900 watt hours.
  3. Allow for losses in the UPS, often around 10 to 20 percent, which leaves roughly 750 to 800 watt hours.
  4. Divide by the load. At 400 watts, that is about 2 hours.

Old batteries hold less, and heat reduces capacity further. For a quick estimate with your own numbers, use our battery backup calculator.

Worked example: a family in Faisalabad

Bilal's home faces about two hours of load-shedding at a time, a few times a day in summer. He wants three ceiling fans, five LED bulbs, the router and the TV on backup.

  • Load: three fans at about 80 watts, five bulbs at about 12 watts, a router at 10 watts and a TV at about 90 watts. That is roughly 400 watts.
  • UPS size: 400 watts at about 75 percent load suggests a unit of about 550 watts or more. A 1000 VA unit with a power factor of 0.7, about 700 watts, would cover it. Whatever the size, he wants a pure sine wave model.
  • Battery: for two hours at 400 watts, a single 12 volt battery would need to be very large. So he moves up to a 24 volt unit, which is usually sold at 1500 VA or more, with two tubular batteries of around 150 Ah. That gives roughly 3,600 watt hours in total, about 1,450 to 1,600 usable after the half depth rule and losses, so roughly 3.5 to 4 hours at 400 watts.

That margin means the batteries are rarely deeply drained, and backup still holds up when they age.

The running cost nobody mentions

A UPS does not create power. Every unit it delivers during load-shedding has to be put back into the batteries from the grid, and charging is not perfectly efficient. With lead acid batteries, you may pay for roughly 1.2 to 1.4 units from the grid for every unit the UPS gives back, plus a small standby draw when it sits idle. Safety basics

  • Have an electrician wire the UPS into a separate circuit for the backed up points. Never connect it by plugging into a socket and back feeding the house.
  • Keep lead acid batteries in a ventilated spot, away from the kitchen, gas lines and children. They release gas while charging.
  • Use proper thick cables and tight terminals. Loose or thin cables heat up.
  • Top up distilled water in tubular batteries when the indicator shows it is low.
  • Do not put the UPS in a closed cupboard. It needs air flow to stay cool.

When a solar hybrid makes more sense

A UPS only moves grid units from one time to another. If load-shedding in your area is long, or you are adding a big battery bank anyway, a hybrid solar inverter starts to look better. It charges the batteries from panels in the day, runs your daytime load from the sun and still backs up the house during outages, so you stop paying extra units for charging. Frequently asked questions

What size UPS for home do I need for 3 fans and 5 lights?

That load is usually around 300 to 350 watts with ordinary fans. A 1000 VA unit is a comfortable choice with room for a TV and router.

Can a home UPS run a refrigerator?

Some can, but a fridge needs a strong starting surge. Choose a pure sine wave unit with a good margin, and check with the maker that it is rated for a fridge.

How long do UPS batteries last?

Tubular batteries often last a few years with daily use and good care. Lithium usually lasts longer. Heat and deep draining shorten the life of any battery.

Does a UPS increase my electricity bill?

Yes. Every unit it supplies has to be recharged from the grid, with some loss, plus a small standby draw. The more load-shedding you have, the more it adds.
