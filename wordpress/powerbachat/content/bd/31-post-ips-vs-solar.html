When load-shedding returns, families across Bangladesh face the same choice: buy an IPS, or invest in solar? Both keep fans and lights running during a power cut, but they work very differently and cost very different amounts. Deciding between an IPS or solar is easier once you see them side by side, so this guide compares them honestly on upfront cost, backup, running cost and suitability, so you can decide what fits your home and budget.

How each one works

An IPS is an inverter with a charger and a battery. When grid power is on, it charges the battery. When the power goes off, it runs selected circuits from the battery. It does not reduce your bill; in fact, recharging the battery adds a little to it.

A solar system uses panels to produce electricity during the day. With batteries and a hybrid inverter, it also stores energy for the evening and for outages. It reduces your bill because daytime electricity comes from the sun instead of the grid.

Upfront cost

An IPS is far cheaper to buy. A typical home IPS with one or two batteries costs a fraction of a rooftop solar system. Current prices are on our IPS price and battery price pages. A solar system with batteries costs considerably more; see the solar system price page for ranges by size.

Backup during load-shedding

For backup alone, both rely on batteries, so the backup time depends on battery size and your load. The difference is how the battery recharges. An IPS needs grid power to recharge, so back-to-back cuts can leave it flat. A solar system recharges from the panels during the day, even when the grid is off, which makes it far more reliable in areas with long daytime outages.

Use our IPS calculator to see backup hours for any battery and load. It works for solar batteries too.

Effect on your electricity bill

This is where the two differ most. An IPS adds to your bill, because every recharge draws units from the grid and some energy is lost. A typical home IPS can add 20 to 50 units a month. A solar system lowers your bill, often substantially, because it replaces grid units during the day. For homes that regularly use more than 400 units a month, where units are most expensive, the saving can be large.

IPS or solar: who should choose which

Who should choose an IPS

  • Flats without roof access.
  • Households with short, occasional outages.
  • Tight budgets where the upfront cost of solar is out of reach.
  • Homes with modest usage, where solar's bill savings would be small.

Who should choose solar

  • Houses with a usable, shade-free roof.
  • Areas with long or frequent daytime outages.
  • Homes using 300 units or more a month, especially above 400 in summer.
  • Families planning to stay in the home for several years, so the system pays back.

Space, noise and safety

An IPS and its battery fit in a corner of a flat, but the battery needs ventilation, because lead-acid batteries release gas while charging. A solar system needs roof space and a safe, ventilated spot indoors or in a shaded area for the inverter and batteries. Neither is noisy, unlike a generator. Both need proper wiring by a qualified electrician, with correct cable sizes, fuses and earthing. A badly wired backup system is a fire risk, whichever you choose.

What about generators?

Some families and buildings still use diesel or petrol generators. They can run heavier loads, such as ACs, but they are noisy, need fuel and regular servicing, and cost a lot to run per unit. For fans and lights, an IPS or a solar system with batteries is quieter, cleaner and usually cheaper over time. Generators make more sense for whole buildings or businesses with large loads.

The middle path: solar-ready IPS

Some IPS units can accept solar panels through a built-in or added charge controller. Starting with an IPS and adding a few panels later can be a sensible step for families who want backup now and lower bills later. Check that the IPS supports solar and that the battery suits daily cycling. Our solar panel price page lists small and large panels.

Running costs over time

Both need battery replacements, the IPS sooner if it uses cheaper batteries. Solar adds panel cleaning and, eventually, an inverter replacement. But solar's bill savings usually outweigh those costs within a few years, while an IPS keeps adding a small amount to every bill. Over five to ten years, solar often costs less overall for homes that can use it, even though it costs more on day one.

A worked example

Two neighbours in Khulna both face two-hour evening cuts. One rents a flat with no roof access and uses about 250 units a month. A 1,000 VA IPS with one tubular battery covers his fans and lights, adds a little to his bill and costs little upfront. The other owns a house, uses about 480 units a month in summer and has a sunny roof. A 4 kW hybrid system with a lithium battery keeps her house running through cuts and brings her summer bill well below the 400 unit line. Both made the right choice for their situation.

The quick verdict

If you rent a flat, face short cuts or need to keep spending low, an IPS is the sensible choice. If you own a house with a sunny roof, use a lot of electricity and face long outages, solar with batteries is the better long-term investment. Either way, size the battery to your real cuts and use efficient fans and lights on the backup circuit.

Frequently asked questions

Is IPS or solar better for load-shedding?

Solar with batteries is more reliable during long outages and lowers your bill. An IPS is cheaper and suits flats and short cuts.

Does an IPS increase my electricity bill?

Slightly, because it recharges from the grid and loses some energy. Old batteries waste more.

Can I add solar to my IPS later?

If the IPS supports solar charging, yes. Check the model before buying panels.

Is solar worth it for a flat?

Usually only if the building allows panels on a shared roof. Otherwise, an IPS with efficient fans and lights is the practical choice.

Which should I buy first if my budget is limited?

Start with an IPS sized for your real outage hours. If it supports solar charging, you can add panels later as budget allows.

How long do IPS and solar batteries last?

Tubular batteries often last a few years; lithium batteries much longer. Panels last twenty years or more.
