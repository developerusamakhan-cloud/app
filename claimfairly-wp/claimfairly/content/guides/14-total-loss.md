The call usually comes a few days after the crash. The adjuster says yours is now a **total loss car**: they will pay you its value instead of fixing it. For a lot of people this is the first time they learn that "totaled" does not have to mean a crumpled wreck. Plenty of cars that look repairable are totaled, simply because fixing them would cost too much compared with what they are worth.

If you are new to claims, our [claim process guide](/car-insurance-claim-process/) explains every step around this one. This guide explains how that decision is made, what the insurer owes you, what happens if you still have a loan, and the choices you have before you sign anything.

## What "total loss" actually means

A car is a total loss when the insurer decides it is not economical to repair. There are two common ways states and insurers draw that line.

**A total loss threshold.** Some states set a percentage. If the repair cost reaches that percentage of the car's value, the car must be declared a total loss. Thresholds of around 70 to 80 percent are common, but each state sets its own, and a few use 100 percent.

**The total loss formula.** Other states let insurers compare costs directly. If the repair cost plus the car's salvage value is more than the car's actual cash value, it is cheaper to total the car than to fix it.

Here is the same car under both approaches:

| | Car A | Car B |
|---|---|---|
| Actual cash value | $12,000 | $12,000 |
| Repair estimate | $8,500 | $9,500 |
| Salvage value | $3,000 | $3,000 |
| Repair as % of value | 70.8% | 79.2% |
| Under a 75% threshold | Repaired | Total loss |
| Under the formula (repair + salvage vs value) | $11,500, repaired | $12,500, total loss |

Notice how close the line is. A hidden-damage supplement found after the shop takes the car apart can push a repairable car over it.

## What the insurer pays

The core of a total loss payout is the car's **actual cash value (ACV)**: what a car of the same year, make, model, trim, mileage, options and condition would have sold for in your area right before the crash. It is not what you paid, and it is not what you owe.

Depending on your state and whose insurer is paying, the settlement may also include:

- **Sales tax** on a replacement car of the same value
- **Title and registration fees**
- **Your deductible taken out**, but only on a claim under your own collision coverage. A claim against the at-fault driver's insurer has no deductible.

A simple example of a first-party claim:

- Actual cash value: $14,000
- Deductible: minus $500
- Sales tax and fees, if your state requires them: plus $1,050
- **Payment: $14,550**

## If you still owe money on the car

When there is a loan or lease, the insurer generally pays the lender first, because the lender is listed on the policy and holds the title. If the payout is larger than the balance, you get the difference.

If you owe **more** than the car is worth, the payout does not cover the whole balance, and you still owe the rest. This is where **gap insurance** helps. It covers some or all of the difference between the actual cash value and the loan or lease balance. Many leases include it. Many loans do not, unless you bought it from the dealer or your insurer.

| Situation | Example |
|---|---|
| Loan balance | $16,500 |
| Actual cash value paid to lender | $14,000 |
| Shortfall you owe without gap insurance | $2,500 |

Keep paying the loan while the claim is open. A missed payment hurts your credit even if the insurer is slow. Our guide to [diminished value after a lease or loan](/diminished-value-leased-car/) covers how lenders and leasing companies fit into the picture.

## Can you keep a totaled car?

Often, yes. This is called **owner retention**. The insurer pays the actual cash value minus what the wreck is worth as salvage (and minus your deductible on a first-party claim), and you keep the car.

- Actual cash value: $14,000
- Salvage value: minus $2,100
- Deductible: minus $500
- **You receive $11,400 and keep the car**

The catch: the car will usually get a **salvage title**. To drive it again, it must be repaired and pass a state inspection to get a rebuilt title. Insurance and resale are harder with a branded title. Keeping a totaled car tends to make sense only when the damage is mostly cosmetic or you can repair it cheaply.

## Your personal things and add-ons

Personal items inside the car, like a laptop or child seat, are generally not covered by auto insurance as part of the car's value. Personal belongings may fall under your homeowners or renters policy, and some insurers pay to replace child safety seats after a crash. Aftermarket parts and equipment are a separate question: some policies cover custom equipment only up to a limit, so tell the adjuster about every upgrade and keep receipts.

## Rental car after a total loss

Rental coverage does not last forever after a total loss. Once the insurer makes a settlement offer, many policies and insurers give a short, fixed window before the rental ends. Ask in writing exactly when the rental will stop, so you have time to shop for a replacement.

## If the car is repaired instead

When the car lands just under the line and gets repaired, it still loses resale value because the crash now appears on its history report. That loss is called diminished value. Our [diminished value calculator](/diminished-value-calculator/) shows what the insurer formula would pay, and our guide on [diminished value claims](/what-is-diminished-value/) explains how to ask for it.

## Your choices when the car is totaled

1. **Accept the valuation** if it matches what similar cars actually sell for near you.
2. **Challenge the valuation** with your own comparable listings and records of recent work. This is where most of the money is, and the insurer's number is often negotiable.
3. **Use the appraisal clause** in your own policy, if it has one, when you and the insurer cannot agree on value.
4. **Keep the car** through owner retention.

How insurers arrive at that actual cash value, and how to push back on a low number, is the subject of our next guide in this series.

[cf_note type="warning"]Do not sign over the title or the release until you agree with the numbers. Once the payment is accepted and the title is transferred, reopening the valuation is very hard.[/cf_note]

## What the rules say

Many states base their claim rules on the NAIC model regulation for property and casualty claims. In general terms, it describes how a total loss should be settled: either by offering a comparable replacement car, or by paying a cash settlement based on the actual cost of a comparable car, with the basis for the amount explained to you. Ask the adjuster for the full valuation report. You are entitled to understand how the number was reached.
