Picture two identical used cars on a dealer's lot. Same year, same trim, same miles. One has a clean history report. The other shows a crash last spring, fully repaired. Which one would you pay more for?

Almost everyone picks the clean one, and that is the whole idea behind **diminished value**. After an accident, your car is worth less than it was, even if the repair shop did a perfect job. A diminished value claim asks the insurance company to pay you for that lost value.

## The three kinds of diminished value

You will see these terms in forums and insurer letters, so it helps to know them.

- **Inherent diminished value.** The loss that comes simply from having an accident on record. This is what most claims are about.
- **Repair related diminished value.** Extra loss because the repair was not done well: mismatched paint, gaps in panels, non-original parts.
- **Immediate diminished value.** The difference in value right after the crash, before repairs. Insurers rarely pay this, because they pay for the repair instead.

## Who pays for diminished value?

In most states, the **at-fault driver's insurance company** is responsible. You are asking them to put you back where you were before their driver hit you, and a car with a lower resale value is not where you were.

Claims against **your own insurer** (first-party claims) are a different story. Many policies exclude diminished value, and only a few states push insurers to consider it. Georgia is the best known, because of a 2001 state Supreme Court case called *State Farm v. Mabry*.

## How much is diminished value worth?

It depends on the car. Newer cars, low mileage cars and more expensive cars tend to lose the most. A ten year old car with 150,000 miles may lose very little, because buyers already expect some history.

Insurers usually start with the **17c formula**: 10% of the car's value, reduced for damage level and mileage. Our [diminished value calculator](/diminished-value-calculator/) runs that formula for you. Keep in mind that 17c is designed to produce a conservative number. We break down why in [The 17c Formula Explained](/17c-formula-diminished-value/).

For a stronger number, many people get an **independent diminished value appraisal**. An appraiser compares your car with similar cars for sale, with and without accident history, and writes a report you can send to the insurer. Appraisals cost money, so they make the most sense when the car is worth enough for the gap to matter.

## How to file a diminished value claim, step by step

### 1. Finish the repair first

You cannot prove the loss until the car is fixed. Keep the final repair invoice, the parts list and before and after photos.

### 2. Confirm the other driver was at fault

Get the police report and the claim number for the at-fault driver's insurer. If fault is disputed, sort that out first.

### 3. Find your car's pre-accident value

Use [Kelley Blue Book](https://www.kbb.com/whats-my-car-worth/), Edmunds or J.D. Power with the condition and options your car had before the crash. Save screenshots.

### 4. Estimate the loss

Run the [calculator](/diminished-value-calculator/) to see the 17c figure. If you think the real loss is much higher, consider an appraisal or gather dealer trade-in quotes that mention the accident.

### 5. Send a written demand

Write to the adjuster, explain the claim and name your number. Attach the repair invoice, photos, your value research and any appraisal. Our [demand letter generator](/demand-letter-generator/) has a diminished value version that does the formatting for you.

### 6. Negotiate

Expect a counteroffer based on 17c. Ask how they reached their number and respond with your evidence. Stay polite and keep everything in writing.

## Common reasons insurers deny diminished value

- **"Your car was repaired to pre-loss condition."** That addresses the repair, not the resale value. The accident history still exists.
- **"Your mileage is too high."** Under 17c, anything over 100,000 miles gets zero. Real buyers do not work that way, and you can show listings that prove it.
- **"You haven't sold the car."** In many states you do not need to sell the car to claim the loss.
- **"Your policy excludes it."** That can be true for claims against your own insurer, which is why third-party claims are usually the better route.

## How long do you have?

Diminished value claims follow your state's deadline for property damage, which is often two to six years. Do not wait that long. Claims are easier while the repair is recent and the adjuster still has your file open.

## Is it worth the effort?

For a newer car with real structural damage, the answer is usually yes. A few hours of paperwork can recover hundreds or thousands of dollars. For an older, high mileage car with a cosmetic repair, the amount may be too small to bother. Run the numbers first and decide from there.
