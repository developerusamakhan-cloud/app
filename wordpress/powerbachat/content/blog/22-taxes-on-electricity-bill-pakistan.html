Many people in Pakistan look at their bill, see the cost of the units, and then wonder why the amount payable is so much higher. The gap is made up of taxes on electricity bill items, plus a few adjustments that are not technically taxes but behave like them. This guide walks through each common line in plain words, explains which ones are fixed and which grow with your usage, and shows you how to check that your own bill is right.

First, a word of caution

Tax rates on electricity are set by federal and provincial law, and they change. The federal budget can change sales tax and income tax rules every year, provinces set their own electricity duty, and NEPRA decides tariff adjustments. So this article explains what each line means, not what it costs this month. For the exact figures, the most reliable source is your own bill, along with official notices from NEPRA and your distribution company.

Where the taxes appear on your bill

On a typical WAPDA area bill, one block shows electricity charges (units, fixed charges and adjustments) and another shows government charges, which lists the taxes. K-Electric bills look different but carry the same kinds of items. Our guide on how to read your electricity bill line by line shows where each figure sits.

The common lines, in plain words

General sales tax (GST)

This is the biggest tax on most bills. General sales tax is a federal tax charged as a percentage of the electricity cost, which includes the cost of units and usually the adjustments. Because it is a percentage, it grows with your bill. The rate is set in the federal budget and has changed several times.

Electricity duty

Electricity duty is a provincial charge, also worked out as a percentage of the cost of electricity. Each province sets its own rate, so two homes using the same units in different provinces can see slightly different duty. It is a small line next to GST, but it still rises with your bill.

TV fee

The TV fee is a flat monthly amount that has long been collected through electricity bills for the state broadcaster. It does not depend on your units. It has been the subject of policy changes, so check your latest bill rather than assuming it is there.

Income tax

Income tax on electricity bills is an advance tax collected from connections whose monthly bill crosses a set threshold. People who are not on the Active Taxpayers List (non-filers) can be charged at a higher rate than filers, and in some cases filers can claim the amount back as an adjustment against their annual tax. Many smaller domestic bills never reach the threshold, so they will not see this line at all. If you are a filer and are being charged as a non-filer, contact your distribution company with proof of your status.

Extra tax and further tax

These two lines mostly appear on commercial and industrial connections. Further tax is generally charged on supplies to people who are not registered for sales tax, and extra tax applies to certain retail and commercial users who are not registered. A normal home connection usually should not show them; if yours does, ask why.

Fuel price adjustment (FPA)

FPA is not a tax. It is a charge per unit that corrects for the difference between the fuel cost assumed in the tariff and the actual cost of generating power in a past month. It can be positive or negative, and sales tax is usually charged on top of it. Our article on the fuel price adjustment explains why it moves every month.

Quarterly tariff adjustment (QTA)

Like FPA, the quarterly tariff adjustment is a correction rather than a tax. It covers changes in other costs of the power sector, such as capacity payments, over a quarter. NEPRA decides it, and it is spread over the units of the following months. It is also proportional to your usage, and GST is normally applied to it too.

Fixed versus proportional charges

The easiest way to understand your bill is to split every line into two groups.

  • Proportional: cost of units, FPA, QTA, general sales tax and electricity duty. All of these rise when your units rise.
  • Fixed or threshold based: TV fee (if charged) and any fixed monthly charge. Income tax kicks in only above a bill threshold, so it behaves like a step.

This matters because every unit you do not use also removes the GST, duty and adjustments that would have been charged on it.

Why two similar bills can be so different

Neighbours often compare bills and find big gaps even when their units look close. Common reasons include:

  • Slab position: crossing into a higher slab changes the rate on your units, and every percentage tax follows it up.
  • Protected status: households that stay within the limit for long enough may qualify for lower rates. Our guide on how protected consumer status works explains the rule.
  • Filer status: non-filers with larger bills can pay more income tax.
  • Province: electricity duty rates differ.
  • Billing month: FPA and QTA change from month to month, so the same units can cost a different amount in June and July.

Take a family that used 300 units in July. Their bill starts with the cost of those 300 units at the slab rate shown in the table. Next comes FPA, worked out on the same 300 units at whatever rate NEPRA set for that month, and then any QTA. GST is then applied to that whole electricity cost, and electricity duty is added as a percentage on top. Finally, any flat item like the TV fee is added.

Now suppose the same family cuts back to 250 units in August by running the AC at 26 degrees and switching off the geyser. They do not just save 50 units at the base rate. They also save the FPA, QTA, GST and duty on those 50 units, so the drop in the payable amount is bigger than people expect. How to check the taxes on your own bill

  1. Download a duplicate copy of your bill. For WAPDA area companies, the PITC online bill portal lets you view it with your reference number.
  2. Note the cost of electricity, the FPA and the QTA in the electricity charges block.
  3. Look at the GST line and check that it is roughly the stated percentage of the electricity cost.
  4. Do the same for electricity duty.
  5. Check whether income tax, extra tax or further tax appear, and whether they should apply to you.
  6. Compare the unit rates with the slab rates on our electricity price per unit page.

Your check will not match to the last rupee because of rounding and deferred amounts. But if something is clearly off, such as further tax on a home connection, take the bill and your CNIC to your customer service centre and ask for a correction.

Can you reduce the taxes?

You cannot opt out of GST or electricity duty, but because most taxes on electricity bill amounts are proportional, using fewer units is the surest way to pay less. Filers should keep their status up to date so they are not charged the non-filer rate. Shop owners may want to ask a tax adviser whether registration would remove extra or further tax.

Frequently asked questions

Is FPA a tax?

No. FPA is a fuel cost correction decided by NEPRA. It is not a tax, but sales tax is usually charged on it, so it raises your tax amount too.

Why do I pay income tax on my electricity bill?

It is an advance tax collected from bills above a threshold. Non-filers may pay a higher rate. Filers can often adjust it in their annual return.

Is the TV fee still charged?

This has changed over time, so the honest answer is to look at your latest bill. If it appears, it is a small flat amount that does not depend on units.

Where can I find the current tax rates?

Your bill shows what was actually charged. For decisions and notifications, check NEPRA, the Federal Board of Revenue and your distribution company.
