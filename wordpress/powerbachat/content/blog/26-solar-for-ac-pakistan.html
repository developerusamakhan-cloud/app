In a Pakistani summer, the air conditioner is the single biggest reason bills climb. That is why so many people ask about solar for AC before anything else. The honest answer is that running one AC in the daytime from solar is very achievable at home scale. Running it all night from batteries is where costs jump. This guide walks through the sizing logic with rough figures, so you can talk to an installer knowing what you need.

Start with the AC, not the panels

Everything depends on how much power your AC draws. An inverter AC does not run at one fixed power. It starts high to cool the room quickly, then slows down to hold the temperature.

As a rough guide:

  • A 1 ton inverter AC typically draws around 1,000 to 1,200 watts when it starts cooling a hot room, and often 400 to 800 watts once the room is cool.
  • A 1.5 ton inverter AC typically draws around 1,500 to 1,800 watts at full speed, and often 600 to 1,000 watts once it settles.

The actual figures depend on the model, the outdoor heat, the room size, insulation and the temperature you set. Setting 26 degrees instead of 22 makes a large difference. If you still have an old non-inverter unit, read our comparison of inverter AC vs non-inverter AC first. A non-inverter unit draws full power every time the compressor runs, which makes it much harder to power from solar.

How many panels for daytime use

Solar panels rarely deliver their full rated output. Heat, dust, the angle of the sun and cable losses all reduce it. In Pakistani summer heat, a panel often produces something like 70 to 80 percent of its rating at midday, and much less in the early morning and late afternoon.

So a simple rule works well: plan for a panel array of roughly two to three times the AC's settled running power. That gives enough margin to cover the starting surge, the morning and late afternoon hours, and the fans, fridge and lights that will share the same system.

With today's common panels of around 550 to 600 watts each, that works out to roughly:

  • 1 ton inverter AC: about 4 panels, or roughly 2 to 2.5 kW of panels.
  • 1.5 ton inverter AC: about 5 to 6 panels, or roughly 3 to 3.5 kW of panels.
  • Two 1.5 ton ACs: closer to 6 to 8 kW, which is why many homes end up with a 5 kW to 10 kW system.

What size inverter

The solar inverter must handle the AC's peak draw plus everything else running at the same time. For a single 1 ton AC, a 3 kW inverter is usually enough. For a 1.5 ton AC with normal household load, most installers suggest 4 kW or more. For two ACs, look at 6 kW and above.

Undersizing the inverter is a common mistake. On a hot afternoon, the AC, a fridge, a water pump and an iron can all switch on together. If the total goes over the inverter's limit, it trips or switches to grid, and the system looks like it is failing when it is just too small.

Night use: batteries or the grid

Panels produce nothing after sunset. If you want the AC to run at night, the power has to come from either the grid or a battery bank. This is the part many buyers underestimate.

Take a 1.5 ton inverter AC running at an average of about 800 watts for 8 hours. That is roughly 6.4 units of energy every night. Lithium batteries should not be drained fully every night, and there are losses in charging and discharging, so you would want something like 7 to 8 kWh of usable battery capacity just for the AC. That is a large and costly battery bank, and it also needs extra panels during the day to charge it.

For most homes on the grid, the practical setup is solar for AC use in the daytime, the grid at night, and a smaller battery only for fans and lights during load-shedding. Hybrid or on-grid

An on-grid system feeds your home from the panels and draws from the grid when solar is not enough. It has no batteries, so it shuts off during load-shedding for safety. It is the cheapest way to cut daytime units, and the AC can use solar whenever the grid is on.

A hybrid inverter works with the grid and with batteries. During load-shedding it keeps running from panels and batteries. If load-shedding is frequent in your area, a hybrid system is usually worth the extra cost, even if you only add batteries later.

Whether you can export surplus units to the grid, and what you are paid for them, depends on current NEPRA rules for net metering and net billing. These rules have been revised and may change again, so check the latest position on the NEPRA website and read our guide to net metering vs net billing before you size a system around export income.

Worked example: one 1.5 ton AC in Multan

Sana's family runs one 1.5 ton inverter AC for about 8 hours in the day and 8 hours at night from May to September. Their other daytime load, fans, fridge, lights and a pump, adds roughly 500 to 700 watts.

  1. Daytime load: about 900 watts for the AC once settled, plus about 600 watts of other load, so roughly 1.5 kW, with peaks near 2.5 kW.
  2. Panels: two to three times the settled load suggests about 3.5 to 4.5 kW of panels. They choose 7 panels of 580 watts, about 4 kW.
  3. Inverter: peaks near 2.5 kW plus margin point to a 5 kW hybrid inverter, which leaves room to add panels later.
  4. Night: instead of a battery bank big enough for the AC, they add a modest lithium battery for fans and lights during load-shedding, and run the AC on the grid at night.

The result: the daytime AC hours, which used to be a large share of their summer units, now come mostly from solar. Their bill still includes the night-time AC units, but it drops by a noticeable amount. Tips that make the system work better

  • Set the AC to 26 or 27 degrees and use a ceiling fan with it. The AC runs at low speed for longer, which suits solar well.
  • Start cooling the room around midday when solar output is strongest, rather than late afternoon.
  • Clean the panels regularly. Dust can cut output a lot in dry months.
  • Keep the AC filters clean and the outdoor unit shaded and clear of walls.
  • Ask the installer for the inverter's rated continuous and peak power in writing.

Frequently asked questions

Can 2 panels run a 1 ton AC?

Not reliably. Two modern panels might cover the AC at midday on a clear day, but output falls in the morning, the afternoon and in haze. Around four panels is a safer minimum for a 1 ton inverter AC.

Can I run an AC on solar without batteries?

Yes, in daytime. An on-grid or hybrid system can run the AC from the panels while the sun is up, with the grid filling any gap. An on-grid system without batteries shuts down during load-shedding.

Is solar for AC worth it if I only use the AC at night?

Much less so. Night-only use needs a large battery bank, which raises the cost a lot. Solar pays back fastest when most of your heavy use happens in daylight.

Will a non-inverter AC work on solar?

It can, but it needs a larger inverter and more panels because it draws full power each time the compressor starts. Switching to an inverter AC usually makes the whole system smaller and cheaper.
