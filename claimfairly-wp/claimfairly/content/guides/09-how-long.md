The honest answer is: it depends on your injuries more than anything else. A simple property damage claim can wrap up in a few weeks. An injury claim usually cannot settle until you have finished treatment, and a disputed claim that goes to court can take years. Waiting is frustrating, especially when bills are piling up, but rushing is usually the more expensive mistake.

Here is a realistic timeline, stage by stage, plus what slows a claim down, what speeds it up, and how to handle money pressure while you wait.

## Typical timelines

| Type of claim | Usual time to settle |
|---|---|
| Property damage only | 2 to 8 weeks |
| Diminished value | 1 to 3 months after the repair |
| Minor injury, clear fault | 3 to 6 months |
| Moderate injury with therapy | 6 to 12 months |
| Serious injury or disputed fault | 1 to 2 years |
| Lawsuit filed | 1 to 3 years or more |

These are rough ranges, not promises. Your state, the insurer, the size of the claim and the evidence all matter.

## Stage by stage

### 1. Reporting and investigation (days to weeks)

You report the claim, the adjuster reviews the police report, photos and statements, and decides on fault. Clear rear-end crashes move fast. Crashes where both drivers blame each other take longer, especially without witnesses or video. Our [after-a-crash checklist](/what-to-do-after-a-car-accident/) covers the evidence that speeds this stage up.

### 2. Property damage (weeks)

Repairs or a total loss payout usually happen early and separately from the injury claim. You do not have to wait for your injury claim to settle to get your car fixed. If the car was repaired, this is also the time to look at a [diminished value claim](/what-is-diminished-value/).

### 3. Medical treatment (weeks to months)

This is the longest stage for most people, and it is the one you should not rush. Most injury claims settle once you reach **maximum medical improvement (MMI)**, meaning you have healed or your doctor can predict what future care you need. Settle before that and you may leave money on the table, because a settlement is final. If you later need surgery that you did not know about, you generally cannot reopen the claim.

### 4. Gathering records (2 to 8 weeks)

Once treatment ends, every provider's records and bills need to be collected. Hospitals and large practices can be slow. This stage often surprises people, because nothing seems to be happening, but the demand cannot go out without complete records.

### 5. Demand and negotiation (1 to 3 months)

You or your lawyer send a [demand letter](/how-to-write-a-car-accident-demand-letter/) with your records and a specific number. The insurer usually responds within about 30 days, often with a lower offer, and the back and forth begins. Each round can take a few weeks. Our guide on [how adjusters calculate offers](/how-insurance-adjusters-calculate-settlement/) explains what they review during this stage.

### 6. Paperwork and payment (2 to 6 weeks after agreement)

You sign a release, the insurer issues the check, and if you have a lawyer, the money goes through their trust account so fees, costs and liens can be paid. Lien negotiations, especially with Medicare or Medicaid, can add a few weeks. See [how attorney fees and liens reduce your settlement](/attorney-fees-and-medical-liens/).

## What slows a settlement down

- **Ongoing treatment** or an unclear prognosis
- **Disputed fault**, especially without a police report or witnesses
- **Missing paperwork:** records, bills or wage proof that has to be chased
- **Medical liens**, especially Medicare and Medicaid, which have their own processes
- **Damages near the policy limit**, where insurers review claims more carefully
- **Multiple injured people** sharing one policy limit
- **Filing a lawsuit**, which adds discovery, depositions and court schedules
- **Slow responses** from either side

## What speeds it up

- Treat consistently and keep every bill and receipt
- Report the claim promptly and get the claim number in writing
- Send complete, organized documents at once rather than piece by piece
- Respond to the adjuster quickly and in writing
- Know your numbers before you negotiate. Run the [settlement calculator](/car-accident-settlement-calculator/) so you can recognize a reasonable offer when it arrives, and the [take-home calculator](/settlement-calculator-take-home/) to see what it would actually leave you

## A sample timeline for a moderate injury

| Month | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0 | Crash, ER visit, claim reported, car repaired |
| 1 to 4 | Physical therapy, follow up visits |
| 4 | Released by doctor |
| 5 | Records and bills collected |
| 5 | Demand letter sent |
| 6 | First offer and counteroffer |
| 7 | Agreement reached, release signed |
| 8 | Liens resolved, money distributed |

Roughly eight months from crash to check is common for this kind of claim.

## Handling money pressure while you wait

Long claims are hard when bills keep coming. A few options can help:

- **Use your own coverage.** MedPay or PIP can pay medical bills now, and your health insurance can cover treatment.
- **Ask providers to wait.** Some doctors and therapists will treat on a lien and wait for payment until the case settles.
- **Settle property damage separately.** Get your car repaired or paid for without waiting on the injury claim.
- **Be careful with lawsuit loans.** Pre-settlement funding can carry very high costs. Read the terms closely and understand how much of your settlement it could take.

## Watch the deadline

While you wait on treatment and negotiation, your state's statute of limitations keeps running. It ranges from 1 year (Tennessee, for most injury claims) to 6 years in a few states such as Maine and Minnesota. Check yours on our [state rules pages](/states/). If the deadline is getting close and you have not settled, talk to an attorney about filing to protect your claim.

## If a lawsuit is filed

Filing a lawsuit does not mean the case will go to trial. Most lawsuits still settle. But filing adds formal steps, each with its own timeline:

| Stage | Typical length |
|---|---|
| Filing and serving the complaint | 1 to 2 months |
| Written discovery (questions and documents) | 3 to 6 months |
| Depositions | 2 to 6 months |
| Mediation or settlement conference | Often within the first year |
| Trial, if no settlement | 1 to 3 years after filing, depending on the court |

Your lawyer may file to protect a deadline, to get access to evidence, or because the insurer will not negotiate fairly. Ask what the plan is and how the fee changes if the case is filed.
