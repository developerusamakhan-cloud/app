A $100,000 settlement usually means a significant injury with strong documentation, often surgery or a long recovery, and a defendant with enough insurance to pay it. Costs and liens tend to be larger at this level, so the detail in your settlement statement matters.

The quick answer: with a one third attorney fee and nothing else taken out, you would keep about **$66,670**. Real settlements usually have case costs and medical liens too, so most people keep less. ## A realistic $100,000 example

Here is a typical breakdown with a one third (33.33%) fee taken from the full settlement, $6,000 in case costs, and a $12,000 medical lien that the lawyer negotiated down by 30%.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Settlement | $100,000 |
| Attorney fee (one third (33.33%)) | minus $33,330 |
| Case costs | minus $6,000 |
| Medical lien after reduction | minus $8,400 |
| **You keep** | **$52,270** |

If the fee were calculated **after** costs instead, you would keep about **$54,270**, a difference of $2,000. That is why it is worth reading your fee agreement closely. Our guide on [attorney fees and medical liens](/attorney-fees-and-medical-liens/) explains each deduction in detail.

## How the fee percentage changes a $100,000 settlement

Before costs and liens:

| Fee | Attorney fee | Left for you |
|---|---|---|
| 30% | $30,000 | $70,000 |
| 33.33% | $33,330 | $66,670 |
| 35% | $35,000 | $65,000 |
| 40% | $40,000 | $60,000 |

## What kind of case settles for about $100,000?

Claims near $100,000 often involve surgery or a serious fracture, medical bills of $25,000 or more, months of lost work and sometimes lasting effects. Policy limits matter a lot here: many drivers carry $100,000 per person, but plenty carry less, which can cap the recovery. To see where your own claim may land, enter your bills, lost wages and injury severity in the [car accident settlement calculator](/car-accident-settlement-calculator/), which also applies your state's fault rule.

## Why fee timing matters more here

With $6,000 in costs, calculating the one third fee after costs instead of before saves you about $2,000. Your fee agreement should say which method applies. If it does not, ask your lawyer to put it in writing before the money is distributed.

## How long a $100,000 settlement usually takes

Claims of this size often take twelve to twenty four months, and many involve a lawsuit, depositions and mediation before they settle. After you sign the release, payment usually arrives within two to six weeks. Liens can add time. Read [how long a car accident settlement takes](/how-long-does-a-car-accident-settlement-take/) for the full timeline.

## Is $100,000 a fair number for your claim?

Run through these before you accept:

- Does it cover all medical bills, including future care your doctor recommended?
- Does it cover lost wages and out-of-pocket costs?
- Is there a reasonable amount left for pain and suffering? Check with the [pain and suffering calculator](/pain-and-suffering-calculator/).
- Did the insurer assign you any fault, and do you agree with it?
- Is it close to the other driver's policy limit?
- After fees and liens, is the amount you keep enough to cover what the crash cost you?

If several answers are "no," read [should you accept the first settlement offer](/should-you-accept-the-first-settlement-offer/) before you respond.

## How to keep more of your $100,000 settlement

- Ask whether your fee is calculated before or after costs.
- Ask your lawyer to negotiate every medical lien.
- Ask for an itemized list of costs with receipts.
- Request a written settlement statement before you sign the release.
- Check whether your own MedPay coverage already paid some bills without a lien.

## Three versions of the same $100,000 settlement

| Scenario | Fee | Costs | Lien paid | You keep |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No lawyer, lien paid in full | $0 | $0 | $12,000 | $88,000 |
| Lawyer, settled before a lawsuit (33.33%) | $33,330 | $6,000 | $8,400 | $52,270 |
| Lawyer, settled after a lawsuit (40%) | $40,000 | $12,000 | $8,400 | $39,600 |

This comparison assumes the same $100,000 gross settlement in all three cases, which is not always realistic. Represented claimants often settle for more, and lawyers usually negotiate liens down. The point is to see how each factor moves your share, not to suggest which path is right for you.

## When the money arrives

A six figure settlement can change your finances, but a large part of it is meant to replace wages and cover care. Consider speaking with a fee-only financial planner before spending it.

Before you sign a $100,000 release, four checks matter most:

1. **Ask whether Medicare paid any of your bills.** If it did, Medicare must be repaid from the settlement, and you should get its final payment amount before the money is split.
2. **Ask what kind of health plan you have.** A self-funded employer plan can be harder to reduce than a regular insured plan, so get the lien amount early.
3. **Get the fee timing in writing.** At this size, before or after costs changes your share by thousands.
4. **Ask whether any part of the payment is interest or punitive damages.** Those parts can be taxable, so the settlement statement should show them separately.

## Fee limits that can change a $100,000 settlement

Most lawyer fees are set by agreement, but some rules cap them. Three examples:

- **Claims against the federal government.** If a federal vehicle caused the crash, such as a postal truck, the claim falls under the Federal Tort Claims Act. Federal law caps the lawyer's fee at **20%** of a settlement reached in the administrative claim stage and **25%** after a lawsuit is filed (28 U.S.C. 2678).
- **Michigan.** Michigan Court Rule 8.121 limits contingent fees in personal injury cases to **one third**.
- **New Jersey and Florida.** New Jersey Court Rule 1:21-7 uses a sliding scale that starts at one third, and Florida Bar Rule 4-1.5 sets a maximum fee schedule for injury cases.

Here is what the federal caps would mean for the example on this page:

| Fee | Attorney fee | Costs and lien | You keep |
|---|---|---|---|
| 33.33% | $33,330 | $14,400 | $52,270 |
| 25% (federal, after a lawsuit) | $25,000 | $14,400 | $60,600 |
| 20% (federal, administrative stage) | $20,000 | $14,400 | $65,600 |

Claims against the federal government also have strict filing steps, including a written administrative claim, usually within two years. Rules change, so confirm the current rule for your state with a licensed attorney.
