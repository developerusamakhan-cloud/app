How to get the right number of units

The calculator is only as good as the units you put in. The best source is the twelve-month history printed on your electricity bill. Add the twelve months together and divide by twelve to get your average. If you mainly want to cut summer bills, use the average of May to September instead. Either way, avoid using a single unusual month.

If you are planning changes, such as a new AC, adjust for them. A 1.5 ton inverter AC used most nights adds roughly 200 to 300 units a month in summer. Our guide on what one unit of electricity is helps you estimate other appliances.

How the solar calculator works

We keep the method simple and open, so you can check it yourself:

  1. Sunshine. We assume around five peak sun hours a day, which is typical for most of Pakistan across the year.
  2. Losses. Real systems lose some output to heat, dust, wiring and the inverter. We allow for 22 percent.
  3. System size. Your monthly units are divided by what 1 kW produces in a month, then rounded up to the nearest half kW.
  4. Panels. The size is divided by the wattage of a typical modern panel and rounded up.
  5. Cost. The size is multiplied by the current installed price range per kW for an on-grid system.
  6. Saving. We estimate your current bill from the slab tariff and assume solar offsets around 85 percent of it.
  7. Payback. The middle of the cost range divided by the yearly saving.

Why the saving is often bigger than you expect

Pakistan's slab tariff charges more for each unit as your use grows. The units solar removes come off the top of your bill, where they are most expensive. That is why a home using 600 units a month often saves more than double what a home using 300 units saves, even though it only needs twice the system. The per unit electricity price page shows how steep that climb is.

What this solar calculator does not include

  • Batteries. The cost is for an on-grid system. For backup during load-shedding, add batteries and use our battery backup calculator to size them.
  • Export income. We do not count money from exporting power, because the rules and rates for new connections have changed. See net metering vs net billing.
  • Fuel adjustments. Monthly fuel charges add to the grid price, so real savings are often a little higher than shown.
  • Shade and roof issues. A shaded or awkward roof may need a different layout or a smaller system.

Daytime use is the key

An on-grid system produces power only while the sun is up. The more of your use you can move into daylight, the more of your bill the system really covers. Run the washing machine, water pump and iron in the late morning. Pre-cool bedrooms in the afternoon so the AC works less after sunset. Families who make these small changes get far more from the same panels, especially under net billing, where exported units are worth less than units you use yourself.

Worked example: a 450 unit home in Multan

A family in Multan averages 450 units a month across the year. Entering 450 in the solar calculator suggests a system of about 4 kW with seven panels. Their current bill at 450 units sits partly in the fourth and fifth slabs. With solar covering most of their daytime use, their bill drops to the first slabs or below in many months. The payback figure lands at a few years, after which the system keeps saving for two decades. If they add one more AC later, they can extend the system, as long as the inverter has room, which is worth asking the installer about upfront.

Should you go bigger than the calculator suggests?

It is tempting to add a few extra panels "just in case". Sometimes that makes sense, and sometimes it does not. Going bigger is reasonable if you are about to add an AC, an electric car charger or a home office, or if your roof has some shade that will reduce output. It makes less sense if the extra power would mostly be exported, because under net billing exported units earn less than the units you use yourself. A good middle path is to choose an inverter with some spare capacity, so you can add panels later without replacing it.

Solar for flats and rented homes

If you live in a flat or rent your home, a full rooftop system may not be possible. Some families install a small system with a hybrid inverter and battery on a balcony or shared roof with the owner's permission, mainly for backup. Others focus on cutting use first: LED lighting, DC inverter fans and an efficient AC. If you are renting, discuss solar with your landlord. A system raises the value of the property, and some owners agree to share the cost.

From calculator to quote

Once you have a size, take these steps:

  1. Check current panel prices on our solar panel price today page.
  2. Read the cost of solar panels breakdown so you know what a fair quote includes.
  3. Get at least three itemised quotes for the size the calculator suggests.
  4. Ask each installer how they would handle shade, structure height and earthing on your roof.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate is this solar calculator?

It gives a sound first estimate using standard assumptions. A site visit can refine it for shade, roof direction and local dust.

What size solar system do I need for 500 units?

Around 4 kW in most of Pakistan. Enter 500 in the calculator for the panel count and cost.

Does the calculator include batteries?

No. It prices an on-grid system. Batteries add to the cost and are sized separately for backup.

Is solar worth it for a small home?

If you use under 200 units and are protected, the payback is slower because your units are cheap. For homes in higher slabs, solar usually pays back much faster.
