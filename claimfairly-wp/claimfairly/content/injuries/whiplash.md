Whiplash is the most common injury in rear-end crashes. Your head snaps forward and back in a fraction of a second, straining the muscles, tendons and ligaments in your neck. It often does not hurt much at the scene, then shows up as stiffness, headaches and pain a day or two later, which is one reason so many people underestimate it.

Insurers see whiplash claims every day, and many treat them skeptically because the injury usually does not show up on an X-ray. That makes documentation more important for whiplash than for almost any other injury. This page explains how whiplash claims are valued, what a realistic range looks like, and what you can do to make sure your claim is taken seriously.

## Common whiplash symptoms

Symptoms vary, but people commonly report:

- Neck pain and stiffness, especially when turning the head
- Headaches, often starting at the base of the skull
- Pain or tightness in the shoulders and upper back
- Tingling or numbness in the arms
- Fatigue, dizziness or trouble sleeping
- Trouble concentrating in the first weeks

If you have severe pain, weakness, numbness that spreads, or trouble with balance, get medical care right away. Your health comes first. The claim can wait.

## Typical treatment and recovery

Treatment usually includes rest, over the counter pain relief, ice or heat, gentle movement and physical therapy. Many people improve within a few weeks to a few months. Some have neck pain or headaches that last much longer, and a smaller number need specialist care.

| Recovery pattern | How it usually affects the claim |
|---|---|
| A few weeks, minimal treatment | Minor: lower multiplier, smaller claim |
| Two to four months of physical therapy | Moderate: multiplier around 2 to 3 |
| Pain lasting six months or more, injections, specialist care | Can move toward serious, with strong medical support |
| Imaging shows disc involvement or nerve irritation | Often valued higher than a simple strain |

## How insurers value whiplash

Most insurers start with your medical bills and apply a multiplier for pain and suffering, then adjust for fault and policy limits. For whiplash, adjusters often lean toward the low end of the range unless the records show consistent treatment and lasting symptoms. They also look closely at the damage to the vehicles, sometimes arguing that a low speed crash could not cause much injury. Low visible damage does not mean low force on your body, but you will need medical evidence to make that point.

Read [how insurance adjusters calculate a settlement offer](/how-insurance-adjusters-calculate-settlement/) for the full process.

## Example whiplash claims

| Claim | Medical bills | Lost wages | Severity | Estimated range |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Six weeks of therapy, back to work in days | $3,000 | $400 | Minor | $7,900 to $9,400 |
| Three months of therapy, two weeks off work | $6,500 | $2,200 | Moderate | $21,700 to $28,200 |
| Six months of care, injections, ongoing headaches | $14,000 | $5,000 | Serious | $61,000 to $75,000 |

These assume no shared fault and enough insurance to pay. Your own numbers may be quite different, which is why the calculator above is the better guide.

## What raises the value of a whiplash claim

- **Seeing a doctor within a day or two** of the crash
- **Consistent physical therapy** with notes on pain levels and range of motion
- **Imaging or specialist findings**, such as an MRI showing disc involvement
- **Clear fault**, like a rear-end collision with a police report
- **Documented impact on work and daily life**: missed shifts, help with childcare, cancelled plans
- **A pain journal** kept from the first week

## What lowers it

- A delay of weeks before your first medical visit
- Gaps in treatment or stopping therapy early
- Prior neck problems in your medical history (these can still be claimed if the crash made them worse)
- Low visible damage to the cars
- Social media posts that seem to show you active and pain free

## Evidence checklist for a whiplash claim

1. ER, urgent care or doctor records from the first days
2. Physical therapy notes, including pain scores
3. Any imaging reports
4. Receipts for medication, braces, heat pads and travel to appointments
5. A letter from your employer about missed work
6. Photos of both vehicles
7. The police report
8. Your pain journal

## Whiplash in no-fault and contributory states

In no-fault states like New York, Florida and Michigan, your own PIP pays your medical bills first. To claim pain and suffering from the other driver, the injury usually has to meet a serious injury threshold, which many whiplash cases do not. Read [at-fault vs no-fault states](/at-fault-vs-no-fault-states/) for details.

In contributory negligence states like Virginia and North Carolina, even a small share of fault can bar the claim, so the evidence about how the crash happened matters a great deal. Check your state on our [state rules pages](/states/).

## Returning to normal activities

Most people with whiplash are encouraged to keep gently moving rather than stay completely still, but every case is different, so follow your doctor's plan. If certain activities are limited, like driving long distances, lifting children or exercising, write them down. Those limits are part of the story that supports pain and suffering. If you had to miss sports, travel or family events, note the dates.

## How long a whiplash claim takes

Most whiplash claims settle a few months after treatment ends, because the records are short and fault in rear-end crashes is usually clear. Waiting until you are released by your doctor is important, because a settlement is final. See [how long a car accident settlement takes](/how-long-does-a-car-accident-settlement-take/).
