A concussion is a mild traumatic brain injury. You do not have to hit your head to get one. A hard jolt that moves the brain inside the skull can be enough, which is why concussions are common even in crashes where the airbag did its job. Symptoms like headaches, dizziness, trouble concentrating, sensitivity to light and sleep changes can show up hours or days after the crash.

Because concussions rarely show on a CT scan or MRI, they are easy for an insurer to downplay. The injury is real, but proving it depends on careful documentation. This page explains how concussion claims are valued, what makes them hard, and how to build a record that reflects what you actually went through.

## Estimate your concussion claim

The calculator is preset to **moderate**. Many concussions resolve in days to weeks, which fits **minor**. If symptoms lasted months, required specialist care or affected your work, **moderate** or **serious** may fit better.

## Common concussion symptoms

- Headache or pressure in the head
- Dizziness or balance problems
- Nausea
- Sensitivity to light or noise
- Trouble concentrating or remembering
- Feeling foggy or slowed down
- Sleep changes, sleeping more or less than usual
- Irritability, anxiety or mood changes

## Warning signs to take seriously

Get emergency care right away for a headache that gets worse, repeated vomiting, slurred speech, confusion, unusual drowsiness, one pupil larger than the other, weakness or numbness, or a seizure. Your health comes first. The claim can wait.

## Why concussion claims are hard to value

- **They are often invisible on imaging.** Diagnosis relies on symptoms, history and clinical exams.
- **Symptoms vary a lot.** Most people recover within days to a few weeks. Some develop lasting symptoms, sometimes called post-concussion syndrome.
- **The effects are often cognitive.** Trouble focusing at work, forgetting things or struggling with screens is real, but it does not produce big medical bills. That can make the multiplier method undervalue the claim. The per diem method in the [pain and suffering calculator](/pain-and-suffering-calculator/) can help show the impact of a long recovery.
- **Symptoms overlap with other conditions**, like stress or poor sleep, which insurers may point to.

These assume no shared fault and enough insurance to pay. For long recoveries, compare these multiplier based numbers with the per diem method.

## Documentation that matters

- **Early medical evaluation**, ideally the same day or next day
- **A symptom diary**: headaches, dizziness, sleep, concentration, mood, screen tolerance
- **Follow up with a specialist**, such as a neurologist or concussion clinic, if symptoms continue
- **Cognitive or vestibular therapy records**, if prescribed
- **Work or school records** showing reduced hours, accommodations or missed deadlines
- **Statements** from family, friends or coworkers about changes they noticed

## What can change your number

- Length of symptoms and whether they are ongoing
- Specialist diagnosis and testing
- Impact on your job, schooling or ability to drive
- Your state's no-fault threshold, if you live in a no-fault state
- The at-fault driver's policy limits
- Any share of fault the insurer assigns to you, under your [state's negligence rule](/comparative-vs-contributory-negligence/)

## Returning to work, school and driving

Many concussion recovery plans involve a gradual return to normal activity. Your doctor may suggest shorter work days, fewer screens, breaks in a quiet room or time off from driving. Ask for any restrictions in writing and share them with your employer or school. Reduced hours, missed classes and accommodations are part of the impact of the injury, and they belong in your claim.

## Talking to an adjuster about a concussion

Adjusters sometimes treat concussions as minor because the medical bills are small. A few tips:

- Describe symptoms specifically: "I get a headache after 20 minutes on a screen," not just "I have headaches."
- Share your symptom diary and specialist notes, not just the ER record.
- Explain how the symptoms affected work, school, driving and family life.
- Avoid downplaying symptoms out of habit. "I'm fine" on a phone call can end up in the claim notes.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- What is my diagnosis, and how long do you expect symptoms to last?
- What activities should I limit, and when can I drive again?
- Should I see a specialist or a concussion clinic?
- What symptoms mean I should come back right away?
- Can you document my restrictions for my employer or school?
