If you put solar panels on your roof, the panels will often make more power than your home needs at noon and nothing at all after sunset. Net metering in India is the arrangement that connects those two halves of the day. Your extra daytime units go to the grid, the units you take back at night are drawn from it, and your bill is based on the difference. This guide explains how it works in practice, what happens to surplus units, how to apply and how it shapes the money you save.

The basic idea

A normal electricity meter counts in one direction only: units you import from the grid. Under net metering, your discom replaces it with a bidirectional meter that records two numbers separately. One is import, the units you take from the grid. The other is export, the units your solar system pushes into the grid when it produces more than the house is using.

At billing time, the discom subtracts export from import. If you imported 300 units and exported 220, you pay for 80 units. If you exported more than you imported, you carry a credit forward to the next bill instead of paying for energy that month. Fixed charges, meter rent and some duties usually still apply even when your energy units net to zero, so a solar home rarely sees a bill of exactly nothing.

Why the grid works like a free battery

The useful part of this arrangement is that you do not need batteries to use your solar output fully. A 3 kW system in most of India produces roughly 10 to 14 units on a clear day, mostly between 9 am and 4 pm. Much of a family's use comes in the evening. Without net metering, the daytime surplus would be wasted or would need an expensive battery bank. With it, the grid stores the surplus as a unit credit and gives it back later.

What happens to surplus units

This is where the rules vary most from state to state, so treat what follows as a general picture and check your own discom's current regulations.

  • Monthly carry forward: in most states, if your export is higher than your import in a billing cycle, the extra units are carried forward to the next cycle and used against future imports.
  • Annual settlement: at the end of the settlement period, often the financial year, any credit still left over is paid out or adjusted at a rate set by the state electricity regulatory commission. This rate is usually much lower than what you pay per unit, often close to the discom's average cost of buying power.
  • Lapse rules: some states have, at times, let unused credit lapse at year end or capped how much can be paid out. Rules have changed several times in several states.

The lesson: size your system to cover your own use, not to sell power, because the annual settlement rate rarely makes extra panels worth it.

Gross metering vs net metering

You may also hear about gross metering. Under gross metering, everything your panels produce is sold to the discom at a fixed feed in rate, and everything your home uses is bought from the discom at the normal tariff. The two are billed separately. Your solar output does not reduce your own consumption at all.

For homes, net metering is almost always the better deal, because each solar unit you use or export offsets a unit you would otherwise pay full tariff for. Some states also offer net billing, where exports and imports are valued at different rates. If your discom offers more than one option, compare them using your own monthly units.

How to apply

For residential rooftop solar, most applications now go through the national portal for the PM Surya Ghar scheme, pmsuryaghar.gov.in, which routes the request to your discom. Some discoms also accept applications through their own websites or offices. The usual steps are:

  1. Register with your consumer number, state and discom, and submit the rooftop solar application.
  2. Get a feasibility approval from the discom.
  3. Choose a registered vendor and install the system, using approved panels and inverter.
  4. Submit installation details and apply for the net meter.
  5. The discom inspects the system, installs or approves the bidirectional meter and commissions it.
  6. If you are claiming the central subsidy, it is released after commissioning. Our guide to the PM Surya Ghar Yojana covers who qualifies.

How long it takes

Timelines vary widely. Regulations in many states set limits for each step, typically a couple of weeks for feasibility and a few weeks for meter installation after you submit the paperwork. In practice, the full process from application to commissioning often takes one to three months, and longer where discoms are short of meters or staff. Until the net meter is fitted and the system commissioned, exports are not credited.

Worked example: a 3 kW system

Take a family that uses about 350 units a month and installs a 3 kW rooftop system producing roughly 360 units a month on average across the year.

  • Daytime self use: about 120 units are consumed directly as they are produced.
  • Export: the remaining 240 units go to the grid.
  • Import: the family still draws about 230 units from the grid, mostly in the evening and at night.
  • Net billed units: 230 minus 240 gives a small credit of 10 units, carried forward.

In summer imports rise with the AC and the family may pay for some units; in winter the credit builds up. Over the year, billed energy falls close to zero, leaving mostly fixed charges.

How net metering affects payback

With net metering in India, your savings depend on the value of each unit you avoid paying for. Because tariffs in India rise in slabs, the units solar removes are usually your most expensive ones, at the top of the bill. That makes the payback period shorter for homes in higher slabs and longer for homes that already pay low rates or get free units under a state scheme.

A few things push payback in your favour:

  • Using as much solar as possible directly during the day, for example running the washing machine or water pump around noon.
  • Sizing the system close to your actual yearly use, so very little ends in the annual settlement at a low rate.
  • Claiming the subsidy, which cuts the upfront cost for small systems.

For many homes using 300 units or more a month, a subsidised system pays back in roughly four to six years, and panels last 25 years. Things to watch for

Net metering in India works well, but a few details catch people out. Read your bill after commissioning to make sure import and export are both shown and the credit is carried forward. Check that sanctioned load matches the system size, since some discoms limit solar capacity to your sanctioned load.

If something looks wrong, contact your discom first. For scheme level questions and approved equipment lists, the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy at mnre.gov.in is the official reference.

Frequently asked questions

Do I still pay anything with net metering?

Usually yes. Fixed charges, meter rent and some duties apply even when your import and export cancel out. Energy charges fall sharply or disappear.

Is the export rate the same as the unit rate I pay?

Within a billing cycle, exported units usually offset imported units one for one. Surplus left at the end of the settlement year is normally paid at a lower rate set by the state regulator.

Can I get net metering without a subsidy?

Yes. Net metering is a discom service and does not depend on claiming the subsidy, though the national portal handles both for homes.

Does net metering work during a power cut?

No. An on grid inverter shuts down when the grid fails, for the safety of line workers. You need a hybrid system with batteries for backup.
