Most people glance at the amount and the due date and put the paper away. That is understandable, but it means missing the parts of the bill that can save you money: the history table that decides protected status, the fuel adjustment that explains surprise increases, and the meter readings that catch errors. This guide shows you how to read electricity bill pages from any Pakistani company, box by box. Layouts differ slightly between companies, but the information is the same.

The top section: who and where

At the top you will find your name, address and the company logo. More importantly, you will find:

  • Reference number. Usually a 14-digit number for the government-owned companies. You need it to view or pay the bill online and for any complaint. K-Electric bills use an account number instead.
  • Tariff category. A code showing what kind of connection you have. Household connections have a residential category. If yours shows commercial or another category, your rates will be different.
  • Sanctioned load. The maximum load your connection is approved for, in kW. It affects your meter type and whether you are billed on peak and off-peak readings.
  • Meter number and connection date.

The meter readings

This box shows the previous reading, the present reading and the difference between them, which is the number of units you are being billed for. It also shows the reading dates. On time-of-use meters, there are separate peak and off-peak readings.

This is the first thing to check every month. Read your own meter when the bill arrives. Your meter should show a number at or a little above the present reading on the bill. If the bill's present reading is higher than your meter, you have been over-billed. If the bill says the reading was estimated, be ready for a catch-up next month.

The history table

Somewhere on the bill, often on one side, there is a small table showing your units and bill amounts for the past twelve months. It is the most useful part of the bill and the most overlooked.

  • It tells you whether you are a protected consumer: if each of the last six months is at 200 units or below, you should be on the protected tariff.
  • It shows your seasonal pattern, which is exactly what you need to size a solar system or decide on a new AC.
  • It reveals sudden jumps that point to a fault, such as a leaking water tank keeping the pump running.

Photograph this table once a year. It is worth more than any advice from a salesperson.

The charges section

This is where the amount comes from. The exact labels vary, but you will usually see these lines:

  • Cost of electricity or energy charges. Your units multiplied by the slab rates. Check this with our electricity bill calculator.
  • FPA or FCA. The monthly fuel price adjustment, a per-unit charge or refund based on fuel costs from an earlier month. See our fuel price adjustment guide.
  • QTA. A quarterly tariff adjustment, when one applies.
  • Electricity duty. A small provincial percentage.
  • GST. Sales tax at 18 percent on the energy charge and adjustments.
  • Income tax. On larger bills for consumers not on the active taxpayer list.
  • Other fixed or surcharge lines. Depending on the company and current policy, a few other small items may appear.

Below these, the bill shows the current bill total, any arrears and the total payable.

Arrears, deferred amounts and installments

Arrears are amounts left unpaid from earlier bills. If you see an arrears line you did not expect, check your payment records. Sometimes a payment made late or at a bank that did not update in time shows up as arrears. Keep your paid bill stubs or online receipts for at least a year. Some bills also show deferred amounts or installments agreed with the company, which are added to the current bill.

Due date and late payment

The bill shows two amounts: payable within the due date, and payable after it. The second includes a late payment surcharge, often around ten percent of the bill. Paying even a day late is expensive, so set a reminder a few days before the due date.

Peak and off-peak bills

If your connection has a sanctioned load of 5 kW or more, your bill probably shows peak and off-peak units separately, each with its own rate. Peak hours are in the evening and change by season. If your peak share is high, shifting the washing machine, iron and water pump to off-peak hours can lower the bill without using fewer units.

A quick monthly checklist

  1. Check that the present reading matches your meter.
  2. Check the units against your own estimate.
  3. Look at the history table for protected status and unusual jumps.
  4. Note the FPA rate by dividing the FPA amount by your units.
  5. Check that there are no unexpected arrears.
  6. Pay before the due date.

It takes five minutes and it catches most errors before they cost you money. If you share the house with family, teach one other person how to read electricity bill details too, so the check still happens when you are away.

When something looks wrong

If the units are wrong, take a dated photo of your meter, the bill and your CNIC to your company's local subdivision office and ask for a correction before the due date. Keep a copy of the complaint. If the meter seems faulty, for example still running with everything switched off at the main, ask for a meter check. For billing disputes that are not resolved locally, companies have a formal complaint process, and the regulator handles complaints that remain unresolved.

Frequently asked questions

Where is the reference number on my bill?

Near the top, usually under or beside your name and address. It is normally 14 digits for government-owned companies.

How do I know how many units I used?

Look at the meter readings box. The units consumed are the present reading minus the previous reading.

What does FPA mean on my bill?

It is the fuel price adjustment, a per-unit charge or refund that reflects fuel costs from an earlier month.

Why does my bill show two amounts?

One is the amount payable by the due date. The other, higher amount includes the late payment surcharge.
