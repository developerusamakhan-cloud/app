Every electricity bill in Pakistan counts "units", but very few people are ever told what 1 unit of electricity actually is. Once you understand it, the bill stops being a mystery. You can look at any appliance and work out what it costs to run, decide which habits are worth changing, and estimate your next bill before it arrives. This guide explains it in plain terms, with everyday examples.

One unit is one kilowatt-hour

A unit on your bill is one kilowatt-hour, written kWh. It is the energy used by something drawing 1,000 watts for one hour. That is it. The meter outside your house simply counts how many of these you use.

So 1 unit of electricity is used by:

  • a 1,000 watt iron in one hour,
  • a 100 watt TV in ten hours,
  • a 10 watt LED bulb in one hundred hours,
  • a 2,000 watt heater in half an hour.

The formula is: watts multiplied by hours, divided by 1,000, equals units.

Finding the watts of an appliance

Most appliances have a label or sticker showing their power in watts (W) or kilowatts (kW). It is usually on the back, the bottom or the charger. For ACs, the label often shows the cooling capacity in tons or BTU and a separate input power in watts, which is the number you want. Inverter ACs and fridges do not draw their full power all the time, so their real use is lower than the label maximum.

How long common appliances take to use one unit

These are typical figures for Pakistani homes. Your own appliances may differ a little.

  • LED bulb (12 W): about 83 hours per unit.
  • Ceiling fan (80 W): about 12.5 hours per unit. A DC inverter fan at around 35 W takes nearly 30 hours.
  • LED TV (80 W): about 12.5 hours per unit.
  • Wi-Fi router (12 W): about 83 hours per unit.
  • Fridge: typically 1.5 to 2.5 units a day, depending on size, age and how often it is opened.
  • 1.5 ton inverter AC: often around 1 unit an hour on a hot night, less in milder weather or at a higher thermostat setting.
  • Water pump (0.75 kW): about 1 unit every 80 minutes.
  • Iron (1,000 W): 1 unit an hour of use.
  • Electric geyser (2,000 W): 1 unit every 30 minutes while heating.
  • Room heater (2,000 W): 1 unit every 30 minutes.

Turning this into a monthly estimate

To estimate an appliance's monthly units, multiply its watts by the hours it runs each day, then by 30, and divide by 1,000. For example, two ceiling fans at 80 W running 14 hours a day use about 67 units a month. An inverter AC averaging 1 unit an hour for eight hours a night uses about 240 units a month.

Add up your main appliances this way and you will usually land close to the units on your bill. If your bill is much higher, something is using more than you think, often a water pump, an old fridge or a geyser.

Why the cost of a unit is not fixed

In Pakistan, the price of each unit depends on how many you use in a month, because the tariff is built in slabs. The first units of the month are the cheapest, and each further block costs more. Taxes and a monthly fuel adjustment are then added. So 1 unit of electricity might cost one price for a small home and quite a lot more for a large one using hundreds of units. Our electricity price per unit page shows the current slab rates.

A practical consequence: the units at the top of your bill are the most expensive ones. Cutting those saves more than cutting the same number in a quiet month.

Small habits, real numbers

Once you can see appliances in units, some choices become obvious:

  • Raising the AC from 20 to 26 degrees can save a meaningful share of its use every night.
  • Replacing four old 40 W tube lights with 20 W LED tubes, used six hours a day, saves around 14 units a month.
  • Switching three old fans to DC inverter fans, used 14 hours a day, saves around 55 units a month.
  • Putting a 2,000 W geyser on a timer for two hours a day instead of six saves around 240 units a month in winter.

Reading units on your meter

Most meters in Pakistan are now digital. The main display, or one of the screens you can cycle through with a button, shows a cumulative number in kWh. That number only goes up. Note it today, note it again next week, and the difference is how many units you used in between. Do this for a few weeks and you will learn your home's normal daily use, which makes any sudden rise easy to spot. Your bill shows the same thing as "previous" and "present" readings. Using units to plan your bill

When you know roughly what each appliance uses, you can plan around your bill instead of reacting to it. If you are close to 200 units and want to stay a protected consumer, you know that one more AC night costs about 8 units, and you can decide. Units and solar

Solar systems are measured the same way. A 1 kW solar system in most of Pakistan produces somewhere around 120 units a month on average. So a home using 500 units a month needs roughly a 4 kW system to cover most of its use, as long as much of that use is during the day. Our solar calculator does this sum for you.

Frequently asked questions

How many watts is 1 unit of electricity?

1 unit equals 1,000 watt-hours, that is 1,000 watts used for one hour, or any combination that multiplies to the same, such as 100 watts for ten hours.

How many units does an AC use per hour?

A 1.5 ton inverter AC often uses around 1 unit an hour on a hot night, and less in mild weather. Older non-inverter units use more.

How many units does a fan use in a month?

An ordinary 80 W ceiling fan running 12 hours a day uses about 29 units a month. A DC inverter fan uses roughly half.

How do I check how many units I have used?

Read your meter and subtract the present reading printed on your last bill.
