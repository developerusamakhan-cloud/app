Rear-end crashes are among the most common accidents on American roads, and they are often among the most straightforward claims. You were stopped or slowing, someone did not stop in time, and the impact came from behind. In most cases the driver who hit you is considered at fault. But "straightforward" does not mean the insurer will offer a fair amount automatically, especially when the injury is the kind that does not show up on an X-ray.

This guide explains how fault usually works in a **rear-end car accident settlement**, the situations where it can be shared, the injuries these crashes cause, and what realistic ranges look like.

## Who is at fault in a rear-end crash?

Every driver has a duty to keep a safe following distance and to pay attention to traffic ahead. Because of that, insurers and courts generally start from the assumption that the driver in the back was at fault. In many states that assumption is close to a legal presumption.

It is not automatic, though. The rear driver's insurer may argue you share some blame if:

- You **stopped suddenly** for no traffic reason, such as missing a turn
- Your **brake lights did not work**
- You **cut in** right in front of the other car and braked
- You were **reversing** when the crash happened
- You were stopped in a travel lane **without hazard lights** after a breakdown

In a chain-reaction crash, more than one driver may share the fault. The car that started the chain usually bears most of it.

## How shared fault changes the number

If the insurer can show you were partly at fault, most states reduce your recovery by your share of the blame. A few states with contributory negligence rules can bar recovery entirely for even small fault. Our guide to [comparative vs contributory negligence](/comparative-vs-contributory-negligence/) covers each rule, and our [settlement calculator](/car-accident-settlement-calculator/) applies your state's rule automatically.

| Your share of fault | Pure comparative state | 51% bar state | Contributory state |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0% | 100% of damages | 100% | 100% |
| 10% | 90% | 90% | Possibly nothing |
| 30% | 70% | 70% | Possibly nothing |
| 55% | 45% | Nothing | Nothing |

## Common injuries in rear-end crashes

The sudden backward and forward motion of the head is what makes rear-end crashes hard on the neck and back, even at low speeds.

- **Whiplash and neck sprains.** The most common injury. Symptoms can take a day or two to appear. Our [whiplash settlement page](/injuries/whiplash-car-accident-settlement/) covers treatment and value in detail.
- **Back injuries,** including strains and disc problems. See our [back injury page](/injuries/back-injury-car-accident-settlement/).
- **Concussions,** from the head moving sharply or hitting the headrest. See our [concussion page](/injuries/concussion-car-accident-settlement/).
- **Wrist and shoulder injuries** from bracing against the steering wheel.

Get checked by a doctor soon after the crash, even if you feel fine. A gap between the accident and your first visit is one of the most common reasons insurers lower offers on these injuries.

## The "low-speed crash" argument

Expect it if your car has little visible damage. The insurer may say a minor impact could not have caused a real injury. That argument is not settled science, and medical records are what answer it. Consistent treatment, a doctor's notes linking the injury to the crash, and your own pain journal carry more weight than a photo of a bumper.

## Worked examples

These use the method in our calculator: medical bills plus lost wages, plus pain and suffering estimated as medical bills times a multiplier for the injury level. They assume you were not at fault.

**Example 1: Neck sprain, six weeks of therapy.** Medical bills $4,500, lost wages $800, minor injury multiplier 1.5 to 2. Pain and suffering $6,750 to $9,000. **Range: $12,050 to $14,300.**

**Example 2: Whiplash with three months of treatment.** Medical bills $11,000, lost wages $2,400, moderate multiplier 2 to 3. Pain and suffering $22,000 to $33,000. **Range: $35,400 to $46,400.**

**Example 3: Disc herniation needing injections.** Medical bills $28,000, lost wages $7,000, serious multiplier 3 to 4. Pain and suffering $84,000 to $112,000. **Range: $119,000 to $147,000.** In this example the at-fault driver's policy limit may matter more than the formula.

Now suppose in Example 2 your brake lights were out and the insurer argues 20 percent fault. In a pure or modified comparative state, the range drops to $28,320 to $37,120.

## What else to claim

Beyond injuries, a rear-end claim against the other driver's insurer can include:

- Car repairs or the car's value if it is totaled
- A rental car or loss of use while it is in the shop
- Towing and storage
- **Diminished value**, the resale value your car lost because it now has a crash on record. Our [diminished value calculator](/diminished-value-calculator/) shows the insurer formula, and our [diminished value guide](/what-is-diminished-value/) explains how to claim it.

## Steps that protect a rear-end claim

1. Photograph both cars, the scene and the traffic signals.
2. Get the other driver's insurance and the police report number.
3. See a doctor within a day or two, and follow the treatment plan.
4. Keep a pain journal and every receipt.
5. Report the crash to your own insurer, then open a claim with the other driver's insurer.
6. Wait until treatment ends to settle the injury part.
7. Send a written demand. Our [demand letter generator](/demand-letter-generator/) can help.
