There is no official price list for car accident injuries. But insurers and attorneys do start from the same basic formula, and once you know it, you can see roughly where a fair offer should land. This car injury calculator runs that formula with your numbers and your state's rules.

## How this car accident calculator works

A typical injury claim has two kinds of damages.

**Economic damages** are losses with a receipt: medical bills (past and expected), lost wages and other out-of-pocket costs. These are added up as they are.

**Non-economic damages** cover pain, stress, lost sleep and the ways the injury disrupted your life. Because there is no receipt for pain, the most common shortcut is the **multiplier method**: medical costs x a number between 1.5 and 5, depending on how serious the injury was.

| Injury severity | Multiplier range | Typical example |
|---|---|---|
| Minor | 1.5 to 2 | Soft tissue strain, a few weeks of care |
| Moderate | 2 to 3 | Months of physical therapy, missed work |
| Serious | 3 to 4 | Fracture, injections or surgery |
| Severe | 4 to 5 | Permanent injury, lasting limits |

The calculator adds economic damages to the low and high ends of the pain and suffering range. Then it applies two real-world limits.

### 1. Your share of fault

If you were partly to blame, your state's negligence rule decides what happens:

- **Pure comparative** (for example California and New York): you lose your share. At 30% fault you keep 70%.
- **Modified, 50% bar** (for example Georgia and Colorado): same, but you get nothing at 50% fault or more.
- **Modified, 51% bar** (for example Texas and Pennsylvania): same, but you get nothing above 50%.
- **Contributory** (Alabama, Maryland, North Carolina, Virginia and DC): any fault on your part can bar the claim.

See every state in our [state rules table](/states/).

### 2. The policy limit

Insurers rarely pay more than the at-fault driver's policy limit. If they carry $25,000 per person and your claim is worth $60,000, the realistic ceiling from that policy is $25,000. Enter the limit if you know it and the calculator caps the result.

## A worked example

Maria was rear-ended in Texas. She had **$8,000** in medical bills, missed **$2,500** in wages, and her neck and back injuries needed three months of therapy (**moderate**). The other driver was fully at fault.

- Economic damages: $8,000 + $2,500 = $10,500
- Pain and suffering: $8,000 x 2 to 3 = $16,000 to $24,000
- **Estimated range: $26,500 to $34,500**

If the other driver's insurer argued she was 20% at fault for braking suddenly, the range would drop to $21,200 to $27,600. At 51% fault in Texas, it would drop to zero. That is why fault is the fight in so many claims.

## What can change your number

- **Evidence.** A police report, photos, witness names and dashcam video make fault harder to argue.
- **Consistent treatment.** Gaps of weeks between the crash and your first doctor visit, or between appointments, give adjusters a reason to discount your pain.
- **Billed vs paid amounts.** Some states limit medical damages to what was actually paid, not the full bill.
- **No-fault rules.** In no-fault states your own PIP pays first, and pain and suffering claims need a serious injury.
- **Your own coverage.** Underinsured motorist coverage can pay the gap when the other driver's limit is too low.

## What to do with your estimate

Use the range as a reality check. If an adjuster's first offer is far below your low number, ask them to explain how they got there. [cf_faq]
[cf_q q="How much is my car accident claim worth?"]A common starting point is your economic damages plus 1.5 to 5 times your medical bills for pain and suffering, then adjusted for fault and policy limits. Every claim is different, which is why this calculator shows a range.[/cf_q]
[cf_q q="What is the average car accident settlement?"]There is no reliable national average, because claims range from a few thousand dollars for minor soft tissue injuries to policy limits for serious ones. Your own bills, injury and state rules are a much better guide.[/cf_q]
[cf_q q="Do insurance companies use a formula?"]Many use claims software that weighs medical codes, treatment length and other factors. The exact rules are private, but the results often land near the multiplier method this calculator uses.[/cf_q]
[cf_q q="Can I get a settlement if I was partly at fault?"]In most states, yes, but your amount is reduced by your share of fault. In contributory negligence states, even a small share can bar the claim.[/cf_q]
[cf_q q="Should I include property damage?"]Property damage is usually settled separately and faster. The calculator shows it on its own line so it does not inflate your injury estimate.[/cf_q]
[cf_q q="Does this replace a lawyer?"]No. It helps you understand the numbers. For serious injuries, disputed fault or a low policy limit, a consultation with a local attorney is usually worth it, and most offer them free.[/cf_q]
[/cf_faq]
