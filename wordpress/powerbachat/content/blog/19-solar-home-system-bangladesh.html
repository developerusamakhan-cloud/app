Bangladesh became famous around the world for its solar home systems: small rooftop panels with a battery that brought light, fans and phone charging to millions of rural households without grid power. Today the grid reaches most villages, so many people wonder whether a solar home system still makes sense. For many families, it does, though often for different reasons than before. This guide explains how these systems work, what they can run and when they are worth buying today.

What a solar home system is

It is a small, standalone setup with three main parts:

  • A solar panel, typically from about 50 to a few hundred watts.
  • A charge controller, which manages charging and protects the battery.
  • A battery, which stores the energy for evening and night use.

Many systems run DC appliances, such as DC lights and fans, directly from the battery. Larger ones add a small inverter for AC appliances like a TV or a regular fan.

What each size can run

  • Around 50 W: a few LED lights and phone charging.
  • Around 100 to 150 W: LED lights, one or two DC fans and phone charging for several hours.
  • Around 200 to 300 W: lights, fans, a small TV and charging, with more evening hours.
  • Larger systems: a fridge or more fans, with a bigger battery and an inverter.

In Bangladesh's climate, each 100 watts of panel produces roughly a third to half a unit on an average day, more in the dry season and less in the monsoon. That is why efficient DC appliances matter so much in small systems.

Why people still buy them

  • Backup during load-shedding. It keeps lights and fans running when the grid is off, and recharges from the sun rather than the grid.
  • Areas with weak supply. Some villages and char areas still have unreliable or no grid power.
  • Shops and small businesses that need light and phone charging in the evening.
  • Lower bills. Moving lights and fans onto solar takes units off your grid bill.

Solar home system or IPS?

An IPS charges its battery from the grid and only helps during outages. A solar setup charges from the sun, so it works even during long daytime cuts and does not add to your bill. An IPS is cheaper to buy; a solar home system costs more but keeps working when the grid is off for long periods. Choosing the battery

The battery is the part that wears out first. Deep-cycle tubular batteries designed for solar are the common choice; lithium batteries cost more but last much longer and deliver more usable energy. Avoid car batteries, which are not built for daily deep discharge. Our solar battery price page lists current options.

Panels and prices

Small panels of 100 to 200 watts are widely available. They cost more per watt than large rooftop panels, but they are the right size for a home system. Current prices are on our solar panel price in Bangladesh page.

A worked example

A family in a village near Sirajganj has grid power, but summer load-shedding often lasts several hours in the evening. They install a 200 watt system with a deep-cycle battery and a small inverter, running four LED lights, two DC fans and phone charging. During cuts, the house stays lit and cool enough to sleep. On normal days, the solar system powers the lights and fans in the evening, which takes a small but steady number of units off their grid bill.

Sizing it yourself

Start with what you want to run in the evening and for how long. Add up the watts: four LED lights at 10 watts and two DC fans at 25 watts make 90 watts. Multiply by the hours you need, say five, to get 450 watt-hours a day. Allow for losses and cloudy days, and you need a panel that produces a bit more than that on an average day, roughly 200 watts in most of Bangladesh. The battery should store at least a day's need within its safe discharge limit: for a tubular battery, that means about twice the daily use in capacity. Our backup calculator helps check the battery side.

DC appliances make a big difference

Small systems work best with efficient DC appliances: DC LED lights, DC fans and USB chargers. They run straight from the battery without the losses of an inverter, and they use far less power than their AC equivalents. A DC fan can use a third of the power of an old AC ceiling fan. If you are building a small system from scratch, choosing DC appliances can halve the panel and battery you need, which is where most of the cost is.

Buying from a reliable seller

Buy the panel, controller and battery from a seller who gives a proper invoice and warranty, and who can explain how they work together. Mismatched parts, such as a large panel with a small controller, waste money and shorten battery life.

Installation and care

  • Mount the panel facing south with a tilt of around 20 to 25 degrees, clear of shade.
  • Keep the battery in a ventilated place, off the floor and away from children.
  • Use the correct cable sizes and a fuse between the battery and the loads.
  • Clean the panel regularly, especially in the dry season.
  • For tubular batteries, check water levels monthly and top up with distilled water.

When a bigger rooftop system makes more sense

If you want to run an AC, a fridge and the whole house, or cut a large city bill, a small home kit is too small. A rooftop system of a few kilowatts, grid-connected or hybrid, is the better choice. See the solar system price in Bangladesh page for those sizes.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a solar home system battery last?

A deep-cycle tubular battery often lasts a few years with good care; lithium much longer.

Can a solar home system run a fridge?

Only a larger system with a bigger battery and an inverter. Small systems are for lights, fans and charging.

Does it work in the monsoon?

Yes, but output falls on cloudy days. Size the battery and panel with some margin.

Can I expand the system later?

Often yes, by adding a panel and battery capacity, as long as the charge controller can handle the extra. Ask about it when you buy.

Is a solar home system worth it if I have grid power?

Yes if you face regular load-shedding, because it provides backup without adding to your bill.
