If you have moved to Tamil Nadu from another state, the first EB bill can be confusing. The units look huge, the bill arrives every two months, and a friend tells you that crossing 500 units "doubles everything". This guide explains TNEB bi-monthly billing in plain words: how the cycle works, why the first 100 units are free, and what really happens at 500 units.

Why the bill comes every two months

Tamil Nadu reads domestic meters and bills households once every two months rather than monthly. It keeps the cost of meter reading and billing down across millions of homes. The important consequence is that every slab limit on the tariff is set for 60 days, not 30. A home using 250 units a month will see about 500 units on its bill, right on the most important line in the tariff.

So the first rule of TNEB bi-monthly billing is simple: before you compare your bill with anyone outside Tamil Nadu, halve the units.

The 100 free units

For domestic consumers, the first 100 units in every two-month cycle are free. That benefit applies whatever your total, so even a home using 800 units in a cycle gets its first 100 units at no charge. For small households, it can mean a very low bill. A family using 180 units in two months pays only for 80 of them.

Two slab tables, not one

Here is the part that catches most people. Tamil Nadu has two different slab tables for domestic consumers, and which one applies depends on your total for the cycle:

[powerbachat_rate_table tariff="in-tneb" plan="standard" caption="TNEB domestic rates per two-month cycle"]

If your total is 500 units or less, the first table applies, including a cheaper rate for units 101 to 200. If your total goes above 500, the second table applies to the whole bill, and that cheaper rate disappears. The 101 to 200 units are then charged at the higher rate, and the slabs above 500 climb further.

What the 501st unit costs

[powerbachat_bill_table tariff="in-tneb" units="480,500,501,520,600" caption="TNEB bills around the 500 unit line"]

Compare 500 and 501 units. The extra unit costs far more than its own rate, because it moves the whole bill to the second table. That is the 500 unit trap, and it is the single most important thing to understand about TNEB bi-monthly billing.

Planning around the 500 line

The good news is that the line is predictable. A few habits help:

  1. Know your cycle dates. Your bill shows the date of the last reading. The next reading falls about 60 days later.
  2. Read the meter around day 40 to 45. If you have used around 350 units by then, you are on course to cross 500.
  3. Act in the last two weeks. Cutting AC hours, moving laundry and ironing, and checking the water motor can be enough to stay under.
  4. Accept the cycles you cannot win. In the hottest months, staying under 500 may not be realistic for a large family. Focus on the shoulder months instead.

A worked example

A couple in Coimbatore with a teenage son has a cycle running from 1 March to 30 April. On 12 April, their meter shows 380 units used. With 18 days left, the pace points to about 540. They decide to use the AC only in the bedroom and only after 11 pm, and they fix a dripping overhead tank valve that was making the motor run twice a day. They finish at 492 units and stay on the cheaper table. In May and June they accept a bill above 500, because the heat makes it unavoidable, but they come back under the line from July.

Why the tariff is designed this way

The two-table structure targets support at households that use modest amounts of electricity, while asking heavier users to pay closer to the real cost. The free 100 units help every household, and the cheaper 101 to 200 slab helps small and middle users. Above 500 units in two months, the board assumes a home can afford a higher rate. It is a reasonable idea, but the sharp jump at the line means households near it have a strong reason to watch their usage.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the units as monthly. They are not. Halve them for a monthly comparison.
  • Checking the meter too late. By the last few days, there is little you can change.
  • Ignoring estimated readings. If a reading is missed and averaged, the next cycle can catch up and cross 500. Photograph your meter on the reading day.
  • Forgetting the water motor. A pump with a faulty float switch quietly adds units every day.

Paying the bill and checking the reading

Every cycle, the assessor records the reading and the bill is generated a few days later. You can see the amount and the reading on the TNEB app or website using your service number, and pay online. Make it a habit to compare the reading on the bill with a photo of your meter taken on the reading day. If they differ, contact your section office before the due date. Paying late adds a surcharge, and a disputed reading is much easier to fix before payment than after.

Comparing Tamil Nadu with other states

Tamil Nadu's tariff is generous to small users, thanks to the free units and low first slabs. Heavier users pay more once they cross 500 units in a cycle. Kerala takes a similar approach with a different line, switching to a single rate above 250 units a month, as explained on our KSEB bill calculator page. How solar changes the picture

A rooftop solar system with net metering reduces the units you import from the grid. For a home that regularly lands at 550 to 700 units a cycle, a small system can bring it under 500 and back onto the cheaper table, which magnifies the saving. Frequently asked questions

Is TNEB bill calculated for one month or two?

For domestic consumers, two months. Every slab limit is set for a 60-day cycle.

Are 100 units free even above 500 units?

Yes. The first 100 units in each cycle are free for domestic consumers whatever the total.

Why is the 501st unit so expensive?

Because above 500 units the whole bill moves to a different slab table without the cheaper 101 to 200 rate.

How can I check my units before the bill?

Read your meter and subtract the reading on your last bill. Use the TNEB bill calculator to see the cost.
