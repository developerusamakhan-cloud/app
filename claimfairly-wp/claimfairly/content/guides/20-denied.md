A denial letter feels final. It usually is not. Many denials come down to a missing document, a disagreement about fault, or a reading of the policy that can be challenged. Some are correct, and knowing why saves you time. Either way, the first step is the same: find out exactly why the claim was denied, in writing.

Car insurance claim denied? This guide covers the most common reasons a **car insurance claim is denied**, how to read the denial, and a step by step plan to challenge it.

## Two kinds of denials

**Your own insurer** denies a claim under your policy, for example collision, uninsured motorist or MedPay coverage. Here you have a contract, and the insurer owes you a duty of good faith in handling the claim.

**The other driver's insurer** denies liability, meaning it says its driver was not at fault, or not fully at fault. It does not owe you contractual duties. Your options are different: negotiate, claim under your own coverage, or pursue the at-fault driver directly.

## 9 common reasons for a denial

### 1. The policy had lapsed

Missed payments can cancel coverage. Check the dates. A cancellation must usually follow notice rules set by your state, and a payment that was in process may matter.

### 2. The driver was not covered

An excluded driver named on the policy, or in some cases an unlisted regular driver in the household, may not be covered.

### 3. A policy exclusion applies

Common examples: using a personal car for business or rideshare driving without the right coverage, racing, or intentional damage.

### 4. The damage is not from this accident

The insurer may say some damage was already there. Photos from before the crash, service records and the repair shop's opinion help here.

### 5. Late notice

Policies require prompt reporting. A long delay can lead to a denial, though many states require the insurer to show the delay actually hurt its investigation.

### 6. Fault is disputed

The other driver's insurer may say you caused the crash or share the blame. In most states shared fault reduces your claim rather than ending it. Our guide to [comparative vs contributory negligence](/comparative-vs-contributory-negligence/) explains the rules.

### 7. Lack of cooperation

Not providing documents, not attending an inspection, or refusing a required statement under your own policy can be grounds for denial.

### 8. Misrepresentation

Incorrect information on the application or claim, such as where the car is garaged or who drives it, can lead to a denial or even cancellation.

### 9. Coverage you did not buy

The most common surprise is collision damage when the policy only had liability coverage, or a rental claim when rental reimbursement was never added.

## Step 1: Get the reason in writing

Ask for a written denial that cites the specific policy language or facts it relies on. In general terms, the claim handling rules in most states, often based on an NAIC model law, require insurers to explain the basis for a denial. A phone call is not enough.

## Step 2: Read the policy section they cite

Get a full copy of your policy, including the declarations page and any endorsements. Read the exact clause the insurer relies on, and its definitions. Insurance policies are interpreted by their exact wording, and ambiguous wording is often read in favor of the policyholder.

## Step 3: Fix what is fixable

Many denials are really "not enough information." If the denial says a document is missing, send it. If fault is disputed, send the police report, photos, witness contacts and dashcam footage.

## Step 4: Ask for a review in writing

Write a short, calm letter that:

- Gives the claim number and date of the denial
- Quotes the reason they gave
- Explains why you disagree, point by point
- Attaches your evidence
- Asks for a written reconsideration by a specific date

Our [demand letter generator](/demand-letter-generator/) helps with the structure. Ask whether the insurer has an internal appeal or review process and follow it.

## Step 5: Complain to your state insurance department

Every state has an insurance department that accepts consumer complaints. A complaint is free, usually filed online, and the department typically asks the insurer to respond. It is especially useful when the insurer has stopped responding or will not explain its decision.

## Step 6: Use other routes

- **Claim under your own coverage.** If the other driver's insurer denies liability, your collision coverage can pay for repairs, and your insurer can then pursue the other side.
- **Uninsured motorist coverage,** if the other driver had no insurance or their insurer denies coverage for its driver.
- **Appraisal,** if your own insurer accepts the claim but disputes the amount.
- **Small claims court** against the at-fault driver for smaller property claims.

## Step 7: Know when to get a lawyer

Consider talking to an attorney if:

- The claim involves injuries and is worth more than a few thousand dollars
- Your own insurer denies a claim that the policy seems to cover
- You believe the insurer is acting unreasonably, such as ignoring evidence or refusing to explain itself

Most states recognize claims against insurers for unreasonable handling of their own policyholders' claims, often called bad faith. The rules and remedies vary a lot by state.

## Watch the deadlines

A denial does not stop the clock. Every state has a statute of limitations for injury and property damage lawsuits, and some policies have their own deadlines for disputes. If time is running short, talk to a lawyer before it expires.

## Keep it in writing

From the first denial onward, keep everything written: letters, emails, the dates of calls and who you spoke with. If you have not been through a claim before, our [claim process guide](/car-insurance-claim-process/) explains each stage, so you can see where yours stalled.
