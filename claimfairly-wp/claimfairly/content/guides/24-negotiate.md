Negotiating with an insurance adjuster is not about being tough. It is about being prepared, specific and patient. Adjusters work from records, formulas and authority limits set by their supervisors. If you give them better records and a clear, reasoned number, you give them a reason to move. If you give them anger or vague complaints, you give them nothing to work with.

This guide gives you an eight step plan for **how to negotiate a car accident settlement**, replies to the tactics you are most likely to hear, and a sample counteroffer you can adapt.

## Step 1: Do not negotiate until you know your numbers

You cannot negotiate a claim you have not valued. Before talking money, finish treatment if you were hurt, collect every bill and record, and add up:

- Medical bills, past and expected
- Lost wages, with proof from your employer
- Property damage, rental and other out-of-pocket costs
- Pain and suffering, estimated with a multiplier or a daily rate

Our [settlement calculator](/car-accident-settlement-calculator/) builds a low-to-high range from these numbers and applies your state's fault rule. Our [pain and suffering calculator](/pain-and-suffering-calculator/) compares both common methods.

## Step 2: Know your walk-away number

Decide privately on the lowest amount you would accept and why. It should cover your economic losses and a reasonable amount for pain and suffering, adjusted for any risk around fault. Write it down. It keeps you from accepting something in the moment that you would regret later.

## Step 3: Send a written demand

Open with a written demand letter that lists your damages, explains why the other driver is responsible, includes your evidence and asks for a specific amount, usually near the top of your range. Our [demand letter generator](/demand-letter-generator/) builds one, and our guide on [how to write a demand letter](/how-to-write-a-car-accident-demand-letter/) explains what to include.

## Step 4: Ask how they reached their offer

When the offer comes back, do not answer right away. Ask for the breakdown in writing: how much for medical bills, wages, pain and suffering, and any fault reduction. Each piece is a separate thing you can respond to. Our guide on [how adjusters calculate offers](/how-insurance-adjusters-calculate-settlement/) explains the logic behind each line.

## Step 5: Counter with reasons, not just a number

A counteroffer should answer the adjuster's reasoning point by point. For each piece you disagree with, give a reason and attach evidence. Then name a number. Move down from your demand in steps, and only when you have a reason to.

## Step 6: Keep it in writing

Phone calls are fine for building rapport, but confirm every offer, counteroffer and promise by email. Written records protect you from misunderstandings and help if you later complain about how the claim was handled.

## Step 7: Be patient

Most negotiations take several rounds. Each round may take days or weeks. Long silences are common. A polite follow-up after one to two weeks is enough. Rushing is the single most common way people leave money on the table.

## Step 8: Know when to stop

Negotiation ends in one of three ways: you reach a number at or above your walk-away point and accept it; the insurer will not move and you decide the offer is acceptable anyway; or the insurer will not move and you look at other options, such as talking to a lawyer or filing a lawsuit before the deadline.

## Replies to common adjuster tactics

| What you hear | What it usually means | A calm reply |
|---|---|---|
| "This is our standard offer for this type of injury." | The offer is based on a formula or software, not your file. | "I understand. My records show more treatment than a typical case. Here they are. Please reconsider based on my file." |
| "Our system calculated this amount." | Claim software produced a range. Adjusters can often adjust within it. | "Which of my records were entered? Please make sure these were included." |
| "There's a gap in your treatment." | They will discount the injury. | "The gap was because of [work, childcare, an appointment wait]. Here is my doctor's note." |
| "This was a preexisting condition." | They will value only part of the injury. | "My doctor's notes show the crash made the condition worse. Aggravation is part of the claim." |
| "The impact was too small to cause injury." | Common in low-speed crashes. | "My medical records link the injury to the crash. Here are the relevant pages." |
| "This offer expires Friday." | Pressure. | "I need time to review it. I'll respond in writing by [date]." |
| "You were partly at fault." | They will cut the total. | "Here is the evidence on fault. Please tell me what percentage you are applying and why." |

## A sample counteroffer

[cf_note type="info"]Claim 7741029. Thank you for your offer of $14,000. I have reviewed it and cannot accept it.
Your breakdown allows $9,000 for medical expenses, but my documented bills are $11,200 (itemized list attached). It allows nothing for lost wages, but my employer's letter shows $2,400 in missed pay (attached). For pain and suffering, I completed four months of physical therapy, and my journal (attached) shows the effect on my sleep, work and daily life.
Based on these records, I am willing to resolve this claim for $38,000. Please respond in writing by May 15.[/cf_note]

Notice what this letter does: it thanks the adjuster, corrects specific errors with evidence, explains the human impact briefly, names a number and sets a date.

## What not to do

- Do not exaggerate injuries or costs. It damages your credibility and can be fraud.
- Do not accept or reject an offer on the phone. Ask for it in writing.
- Do not sign a release before you agree with every term.
- Do not post about the accident or your recovery on social media.
- Do not let the statute of limitations run out while you negotiate.

## If negotiation stalls

If the adjuster stops moving and the gap is large, ask to speak with a supervisor, file a complaint with your state insurance department if the insurer is not responding, or consult a lawyer.
