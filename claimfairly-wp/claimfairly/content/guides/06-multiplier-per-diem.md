There is no bill for sleepless nights, missed soccer games, or the fear you feel merging onto the highway now. But pain and suffering is a real, recognized part of an injury claim, and in many claims it is the largest part. The challenge is turning something personal into a number an insurance company will take seriously.

There are two common ways to do it: the **multiplier method** and the **per diem method**. This guide explains both, shows worked examples, compares them side by side, and covers the evidence that makes either number believable.

## What pain and suffering covers

Pain and suffering is part of what lawyers call **non-economic damages**: losses that do not come with a receipt. It can include:

- Physical pain, during treatment and after
- Emotional distress, anxiety, fear of driving, trouble sleeping
- Loss of enjoyment of life: hobbies, sports, travel, time with family
- Inconvenience and disruption of your daily routine
- Scarring, disfigurement or lasting physical limits

Economic damages (medical bills, lost wages, repairs) are added up from paperwork. Non-economic damages have to be estimated, which is why the methods below exist.

## The multiplier method

**Pain and suffering = medical expenses x a multiplier (usually 1.5 to 5)**

The multiplier rises with the seriousness and duration of the injury:

| Injury | Typical multiplier |
|---|---|
| Minor: strains, bruises, a few weeks of care | 1.5 to 2 |
| Moderate: months of therapy, missed work | 2 to 3 |
| Serious: fractures, injections, surgery | 3 to 4 |
| Severe: permanent injury or disability | 4 to 5 |

**Example:** $12,000 in medical bills for a moderate injury. $12,000 x 2 to 3 = **$24,000 to $36,000**.

**Why insurers like it:** it ties pain to something measurable and easy to verify, namely how much treatment you needed. **Where it falls short:** if you had a painful injury but little expensive treatment, the result can be unfairly low. It can also be distorted by billing rates that vary widely between providers.

### What moves the multiplier up or down

- **Up:** objective findings on imaging, surgery or injections, a long recovery, permanent effects, a strong effect on work or family life, and clear fault on the other driver.
- **Down:** gaps in treatment, soft tissue injuries only, a quick recovery, prior injuries to the same area, or doubt about how the crash happened.

## The per diem method

**Pain and suffering = daily rate x number of days in recovery**

"Per diem" is Latin for "per day." The daily rate is often based on your daily earnings, on the idea that dealing with pain for a day is at least as hard as working a day.

**Example:** you earn $52,000 a year, about $200 per working day, and it took 150 days to recover. $200 x 150 = **$30,000**.

To estimate a daily rate from salary, divide your yearly income by about 260 working days. Our [pain and suffering calculator](/pain-and-suffering-calculator/) does this for you when you enter your income.

**Why it helps:** it reflects how long you suffered, not just what the treatment cost. **Where it falls short:** it breaks down for permanent injuries with no end date, and adjusters often push back on the daily rate or the number of days.

### Choosing the number of days

Most people count from the day of the crash to the day their doctor released them or said they reached maximum medical improvement. Keep records that support the date: a discharge note from physical therapy, a final visit summary or a return to work letter.

## Side by side: the same injury, two methods

Take a broken wrist with **$10,000** in medical bills, a **serious** severity, a person earning **$60,000** a year, and **120 days** of recovery.

| Method | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Multiplier (low) | $10,000 x 3 | $30,000 |
| Multiplier (high) | $10,000 x 4 | $40,000 |
| Per diem | $231 x 120 days | $27,720 |

Here the two methods land close together, which makes the number easier to defend.

Now take whiplash with **$3,500** in bills, **moderate** severity, the same income and **180 days** of lingering neck pain:

| Method | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Multiplier | $3,500 x 2 to 3 | $7,000 to $10,500 |
| Per diem | $231 x 180 days | $41,580 |

Here the methods are far apart. The per diem number better reflects six months of pain, but expect strong pushback from the adjuster. A realistic demand might sit somewhere between, supported by a pain journal and therapy notes.

## Which one should you use?

| If your claim looks like this | Try this method |
|---|---|
| Big medical bills, quick recovery | Multiplier |
| Small bills, long recovery | Per diem |
| Permanent injury | Multiplier |
| Both methods land close | Use either and say so |

You do not have to pick only one. Many people calculate both, see where they land and use the range to set a reasonable demand. Then they add economic damages and adjust for fault in the full [car accident settlement calculator](/car-accident-settlement-calculator/).

## Making your number believable

Adjusters do not pay for pain they cannot see. The evidence that helps most:

- **Medical records** that describe pain levels and limits, not just diagnoses
- **A pain journal** with short daily notes about pain, sleep and what you could not do
- **Photos** of injuries, casts, braces and equipment
- **Things you missed:** work, trips, family events, workouts, school
- **Statements** from family, friends or coworkers about how you changed
- **Prescriptions** for pain medication, sleep aids or counseling

Specific beats dramatic. "I couldn't lift my daughter for eight weeks" is more convincing than "my life was ruined." Put these details in your [demand letter](/how-to-write-a-car-accident-demand-letter/), where they support the number you ask for.

## Things that can limit pain and suffering

- **No-fault states** may require a serious injury before you can claim it at all. See [at-fault vs no-fault states](/at-fault-vs-no-fault-states/).
- **Some states cap** non-economic damages in certain types of cases, although most caps do not apply to ordinary car accident claims.
- **Shared fault** reduces it like any other damages, and in Michigan it is barred entirely above 50% fault.
- **Policy limits** cap the total, no matter what the pain is worth.

## How to present pain and suffering in a demand letter

A number on its own is easy to dismiss. A short, specific paragraph gives it weight. For example:

> For about four months after the crash I could not turn my head without pain. I stopped coaching my son's soccer team, needed help carrying groceries, and woke up most nights from neck pain. My physical therapist's notes from weeks 2, 6 and 10 record pain levels of 7, 5 and 3 out of 10. Based on medical expenses of $12,000 and a multiplier of 2.5, I value my pain and suffering at $30,000.

That paragraph combines daily details, medical evidence and a clear method. Our [demand letter generator](/demand-letter-generator/) gives you a place to add it.
