Opening a high electricity bill when nothing at home seems to have changed is stressful. The good news is that most sudden jumps have an ordinary explanation, and the ones that are genuine mistakes can usually be corrected if you complain in the right way. This guide walks through the usual causes, a few simple checks you can do yourself, and the route to escalate a complaint with your discom.

Start with the bill itself

Before blaming the meter for a high electricity bill, look closely at three things on the bill: the billing period, the previous and current readings, and the reading type. Many bills have a small code or note showing whether the reading was actual, estimated or provisional. Also check the number of days covered. A bill for 40 or 45 days will naturally look bigger than a normal 30 day bill, and on a slab tariff it can also push you into a higher rate.

Common reasons a bill jumps

An estimated reading followed by an actual one

If the meter reader could not access your meter, the discom may have sent an estimated reading based on your past average. When an actual reading is finally taken, all the units that were under counted land on one bill. Your total use over the months has not changed, but one bill carries the catch up. Some discoms spread the units across the months correctly; others bill them all at once at a higher slab, which is worth disputing.

Crossing a slab

Indian tariffs charge more per unit as use rises. A small increase in units can produce a large jump in the bill if it pushes you over a threshold. In some states, crossing a limit changes the rate for all units or removes a subsidy. Our article on TNEB bi-monthly billing shows how one such threshold works in Tamil Nadu.

The season

Summer is the usual culprit. ACs, coolers, fridges working harder and water pumps running longer can easily double consumption between March and June. In the north, winter brings geysers and room heaters. Compare your bill with the same month last year, not with last month.

A faulty or ageing appliance

A fridge with a worn door seal, an AC with low gas, a geyser with a failed thermostat or a water pump with a leaking foot valve can quietly run for hours more than it should. These faults rarely announce themselves. The bill is often the first sign.

Earth leakage in the wiring

Damaged insulation, damp walls or a faulty appliance can leak current to earth. Depending on how your wiring and meter are arranged, that leaked current may be recorded as consumption. Earth leakage is also a safety risk. If your home does not have an RCCB, or it keeps tripping, get an electrician to test the wiring.

A faulty meter

Meters can fail, run fast, or be read wrongly. This is less common than the reasons above, but it does happen, especially after a lightning strike, a voltage surge or when an old meter is replaced with a new one.

A tariff revision or new charges

State regulators revise tariffs, usually once a year, and discoms add fuel and power purchase adjustment charges that can change from month to month. If your units are similar but the amount is higher, compare the rates with an older bill, or see the state rates on our India electricity bill pages.

Arrears and adjustments

A missed payment, a late fee, a cheque that bounced or a back billing adjustment from an audit will show as arrears. Look for a line marked arrears, previous dues or adjustment.

How to check your meter at home

You can do a simple test in about an hour.

  1. Write down the current kWh reading shown on the meter display.
  2. Switch off every appliance in the house, or switch off the main breaker inside the house after the meter.
  3. Watch the meter. On most digital meters, a small LED marked imp/kWh or a similar label blinks as units are recorded. With everything off, it should stop blinking and the reading should not change.
  4. If the LED keeps blinking with the load off, you may have leakage in your wiring or a meter fault. Call an electrician first, then the discom.
  5. Next, switch on one appliance whose wattage you know, such as a 1000 W heater, for exactly one hour. The reading should rise by about one unit.

Worked example: is the bill really wrong?

Suppose a family in Bengaluru usually uses around 180 units a month. Their June bill shows 420 units and a much higher amount. The bill note says the April and May readings were estimated at 180 units each.

The actual meter reading in June shows that true use over the three summer months was about 780 units, roughly 260 a month, as the AC came on. The June bill has absorbed the 160 units that were under estimated in the earlier two months. The family did use those units, so the total is fair, but billing 420 units in a single month may push many of them into a higher slab than if they had been spread out.

How to raise a complaint with the discom

Most discoms accept billing complaints through a helpline, a website or app, a local section office, or in writing. Whatever route you use, get a complaint number.

  • State your consumer number, the bill date and amount, and what you believe is wrong.
  • Attach a photo of the meter showing the current reading, and copies of recent bills.
  • Ask for a meter test if you suspect a fault. Many discoms charge a small testing fee, refunded if the meter is found faulty.
  • Ask whether you can pay an undisputed amount, such as your average bill, while the complaint is reviewed. Rules on this vary by state, but paying something reduces the risk of disconnection.

Keep copies of everything and note the dates of every call and visit.

Escalating to the CGRF and ombudsman

If the discom does not resolve the complaint within the time it promises, or you are unhappy with its answer, you can take it to the consumer grievance forum. Every discom is required to have a Consumer Grievance Redressal Forum, usually called the CGRF. It hears complaints about billing, meters, connections and supply, and it can order the discom to correct a bill.

If the CGRF does not settle the matter, the next step is the electricity ombudsman appointed by your state electricity regulatory commission. Time limits, forms and fees, if any, differ from state to state, so check your discom's bill, website or the commission's site for the current procedure and addresses.

Preventing the next surprise

Note your meter reading on the same date each month. It takes a minute and lets you spot an estimated reading, a fault or a sudden rise early. When replacing old appliances, look for a good BEE star rating; the Bureau of Energy Efficiency explains the labelling scheme. A high electricity bill is much easier to dispute when you have your own record of readings.

Frequently asked questions

Should I pay a bill I think is wrong?

It is usually safest to pay the undisputed part, or the full amount under protest, while the complaint is reviewed. Check your discom's rules to avoid disconnection or late fees.

Can the discom charge for months of old units at once?

Discoms can recover units that were genuinely under billed, but you can ask for them to be spread over the correct months so you are not pushed into a higher slab unfairly.

How do I know if my meter is running fast?

Switch everything off and check the LED stops blinking, then run a known load for an hour. If the results look wrong, request an official meter test.

Who decides a dispute if the discom refuses?

First the CGRF set up by the discom, and after that the electricity ombudsman under your state commission.
