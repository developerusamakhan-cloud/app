Lawyer ads make it sound like every fender bender needs an attorney. Insurers sometimes make it sound like no one does. The truth is in the middle. Plenty of claims are simple enough to handle yourself, and plenty are not. The question is not whether anyone ever needs a lawyer for a car insurance claim, but whether a lawyer is likely to add more to your claim than they take in fees.

We are not lawyers and we do not refer people to lawyers. This guide is an honest checklist to help you decide, with the math to compare both paths.

## You can probably handle it yourself if

- **Nobody was hurt,** or injuries were minor and you have fully recovered
- **Fault is clear** and the other driver's insurer has accepted it
- **Medical bills are small,** a few thousand dollars or less
- **The offer covers your costs** and falls inside a realistic range
- **You are comfortable** writing letters and negotiating in writing

Property-only claims especially are often handled without a lawyer. Our free tools help: the [settlement calculator](/car-accident-settlement-calculator/) estimates a range, and the [demand letter generator](/demand-letter-generator/) writes the letter.

## You should seriously consider a lawyer if

- **The injuries are serious:** fractures, surgery, a head injury, or anything with lasting effects
- **Fault is disputed,** or the insurer says you share the blame
- **Damages may exceed the policy limit,** and you need to find other coverage
- **The claim was denied** and the reasons seem wrong
- **There is an uninsured or underinsured driver,** and you are claiming under your own policy
- **A government vehicle was involved,** where special notice deadlines can be very short
- **A commercial vehicle was involved,** such as a truck or delivery van
- **Large liens** from health insurers, Medicare or Medicaid need to be negotiated
- **The statute of limitations is getting close**
- **You feel pressured,** confused or unable to deal with the claim while recovering

## How lawyers are paid

Most car accident lawyers work on a **contingency fee**: they take a percentage of the settlement and nothing if you recover nothing. A common arrangement is about one-third if the case settles before a lawsuit, and around 40 percent if it goes to litigation. Case costs, such as records, expert fees and filing fees, are usually separate and come out of the settlement too.

Fees must be reasonable and, for contingency arrangements, put in writing under the ethics rules most states follow. Percentages vary, and they can be negotiated, especially for clear claims.

## The math: does a lawyer pay for themselves?

The only fair comparison is what lands in your pocket.

**Path A: Handle it yourself.** You negotiate and settle for $30,000. No fee. Your take-home is **$30,000** before any liens.

**Path B: Hire a lawyer.** The lawyer negotiates $50,000. One-third fee: $16,650. Costs: $1,500. Your take-home is **$31,850** before liens.

In this example the lawyer's work roughly pays for itself, and you avoided the stress. If the lawyer had only gotten $40,000, your take-home would be about $25,180, less than doing it yourself. If they had gotten $80,000, it would be about $51,860.

Where lawyers tend to add the most value is in serious injury claims, disputed fault, finding extra insurance, and reducing liens. In small, clear claims, the fee can eat up most of the difference. Run your own numbers with our [take-home calculator](/settlement-calculator-take-home/), and see our guide on [attorney fees and medical liens](/attorney-fees-and-medical-liens/) for how the deductions work.

## What a good lawyer actually does

- Investigates fault and gathers evidence you cannot easily get
- Finds every source of insurance, including policies you did not know about
- Values the claim with experience from similar cases
- Handles all communication with the insurer
- Negotiates down medical liens, which can raise your take-home a lot
- Files a lawsuit before the deadline if the insurer will not pay fairly

## Questions to ask in a free consultation

Most car accident lawyers offer a free first meeting. Bring your documents and ask:

1. What percentage do you charge, and does it change if we file a lawsuit?
2. Is the fee calculated before or after case costs are subtracted?
3. Who pays the costs if we lose?
4. Who will actually work on my case day to day?
5. How many car accident cases like mine have you handled recently?
6. What do you think my case is worth, and why?
7. Will you try to reduce my medical liens?
8. How will you keep me updated, and how often?

Get the fee agreement in writing and read it before signing.

## A middle path

You do not have to decide on day one. Many people start the claim themselves, gather records and see the first offer. If the offer is fair, they settle. If it is far too low, or if things get complicated, they talk to a lawyer then. The main risks of waiting are missing a deadline and saying something in a recorded statement that hurts the claim, so be careful with both.

Note that once you have a lawyer, the fee usually applies to the whole settlement, including any amount the insurer had already offered. Some lawyers will agree to charge only on the increase over an existing offer. It is worth asking.

## When the claim gets denied

A denial is one of the clearest signs that professional help may be worth it, especially for injury claims or when your own insurer denies coverage. Our guide on [what to do when a car insurance claim is denied](/car-insurance-claim-denied/) covers the steps you can take first.
