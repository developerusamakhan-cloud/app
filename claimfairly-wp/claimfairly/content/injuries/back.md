Back injuries cover a wide range. A lower back strain might clear up in a few weeks with rest and stretching. A herniated disc can mean months of therapy, injections or surgery, and pain that changes how you work, sleep and move. The value of a back injury claim follows that same range, which is why two people with "back pain after a car accident" can end up with very different settlements.

This page explains the common types of back injuries after a crash, how they are usually treated, how insurers value them, and what evidence makes the biggest difference.

## Common back injuries after a crash

| Injury | Typical treatment | Usual severity range |
|---|---|---|
| Muscle strain or sprain | Rest, medication, physical therapy | Minor to moderate |
| Herniated or bulging disc | Therapy, injections, sometimes surgery | Moderate to serious |
| Compression fracture | Bracing, pain control, sometimes surgery | Serious |
| Nerve involvement (such as sciatica) | Therapy, injections, specialist care | Moderate to serious |
| Spinal cord injury | Hospital care, rehabilitation, long-term support | Severe |

Many back strains improve within weeks. Disc injuries and nerve problems can take months, and some people have long-term pain. Your doctor is the best guide to your own recovery.

## Symptoms worth reporting

- Pain in the lower, middle or upper back
- Pain that spreads into the buttocks or legs
- Numbness, tingling or weakness in the legs
- Stiffness that limits bending, lifting or sitting
- Pain that worsens at night or with certain movements

Sudden weakness, loss of bladder or bowel control, or numbness in the groin area need emergency care.

## How insurers look at back injuries

- **Imaging.** An MRI showing a disc herniation is strong evidence. Keep in mind that many adults have disc changes without symptoms, and insurers know it. They may argue a finding was there before the crash.
- **Timing.** Pain and treatment that started right after the crash connect the injury to it.
- **Prior back problems.** Earlier injuries do not end your claim. If the crash made an existing condition worse, that worsening can be claimed, though your earlier records will be reviewed.
- **Treatment path.** Therapy, then injections, then surgery shows a real escalation. Each step supports a higher value.
- **Work impact.** Back injuries often affect jobs that involve lifting, standing or driving, and lost wages can be significant.

## Example back injury claims

| Claim | Medical bills | Lost wages | Severity | Estimated range |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lower back strain, eight weeks of therapy | $4,500 | $1,000 | Minor | $12,250 to $14,500 |
| Disc bulge, four months of therapy, one injection | $12,000 | $4,000 | Moderate | $40,000 to $52,000 |
| Herniated disc with surgery | $45,000 | $15,000 | Serious | $195,000 to $240,000 |

These assume no shared fault. In practice, the surgery example would often be limited by the at-fault driver's policy limit, which is why entering the limit in the calculator matters.

## What can change your number

- **Surgery** usually moves a claim into the serious range
- **Missed work** and any change to the kind of work you can do
- **Future care** your doctor says you will need, such as more injections or a future surgery
- **Fault and your state's rules**, which the calculator applies
- **The at-fault driver's policy limit**, which often caps back injury claims in practice
- **Your own coverage**, such as underinsured motorist coverage, when the other policy is too small

## Evidence checklist for a back injury claim

1. First medical visit records, ideally within a few days of the crash
2. Imaging reports (X-ray, MRI, CT)
3. Physical therapy notes with pain scores and function limits
4. Specialist and injection records
5. A doctor's note about future treatment or work restrictions
6. Employer letter about missed work or light duty
7. Pain journal and photos of braces or equipment
8. Records from before the crash, if you had earlier back issues

## Back injuries and the per diem method

Back injuries often involve long recoveries with modest monthly bills. In those cases the per diem method can better reflect the months of pain. ## Returning to work with a back injury

Back injuries often affect work more than other injuries, especially jobs that involve lifting, driving, standing or long hours at a desk. If your doctor puts you on light duty or limits your hours, ask for the restrictions in writing and give a copy to your employer. Lost pay from reduced hours counts as lost wages, not just full days off. If your injury means you cannot return to the same kind of work, that loss of earning capacity can be a significant part of the claim and is worth discussing with an attorney.

## Questions to ask your doctor that also help your claim

- What is my diagnosis, in plain words, and what caused it?
- How long do you expect recovery to take?
- Will I need injections, surgery or more therapy in the future?
- What activities should I avoid, and for how long?
- Can you note my work restrictions in writing?
- Is this condition likely to cause problems later, like arthritis or recurring pain?

Your doctor's answers guide your recovery first. They also become the medical evidence that supports future costs and pain and suffering in your claim.

## Costs people forget with back injuries

- Over the counter pain relievers, heat pads, braces and supportive pillows
- Mileage and parking for therapy visits, which can add up over months
- Help with household tasks you could not do, like lawn care or cleaning
- A new mattress or chair if your doctor recommended one
