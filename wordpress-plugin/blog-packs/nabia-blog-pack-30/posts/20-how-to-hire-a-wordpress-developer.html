Quick answer: to hire a WordPress developer, define the job, write a one-page brief, shortlist three to five candidates, check their live work, ask about speed, security, ownership and support, compare scope rather than price, and start with a written agreement. The whole process usually takes one to two weeks.

Hiring a developer feels risky when you cannot judge code yourself. You do not need to. You need a good process, and the right questions do most of the work for you.

Step 1: Define the job

New website, redesign, store, fix, speed-up, migration or ongoing care? The skills differ. If you are not sure what kind of help you need, read WordPress expert vs WordPress developer.

Step 2: Write a one-page brief

  • Your business in two sentences and your ideal customer
  • The goal of the site: calls, bookings, sales, credibility
  • Pages and features you need
  • Websites you like and why
  • Budget range and deadline

Step 3: Find three to five candidates

Ask business friends for referrals, look at sites you admire and check who built them, or search Upwork and Fiverr where reviews are verified. Contact more than one, but not twenty.

Step 4: Check live work

Open their sites on your phone. Are they fast? Clear? Do the forms work? Screenshots can hide a lot; live sites cannot.

Step 5: Ask the right questions

  1. Which theme or builder would you use, and why?
  2. How will I edit the site after launch?
  3. How do you handle speed, security and backups?
  4. Will the domain, hosting and site be in my name?
  5. What exactly is included, and what costs extra?
  6. What support do I get after launch?

Step 6: Compare scope, not just price

Put the quotes side by side: pages, revisions, content help, SEO setup, training and support. The cheapest quote often leaves out the parts that make a site work. Typical freelance rates run from $25 to $100 per hour; many developers prefer fixed prices for clear projects.

Step 7: Start with a clear agreement

Scope, price, payment milestones (often 50 percent upfront and 50 percent at launch), timeline, revisions, ownership and support. Short is fine, as long as it is written down.

Red flags at any step

  • No live work, only mockups
  • No questions about your business
  • Pressure to decide quickly
  • Hosting or domain in their name
  • Slow, vague replies before you have even paid
