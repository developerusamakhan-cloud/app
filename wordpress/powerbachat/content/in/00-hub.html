How a bill is worked out in India

The basic idea is the same in every state. The meter counts the units (kilowatt-hours) used in the billing period. Those units are split into slabs, and each slab is charged at its own rate. On top of that come a fixed charge, usually based on your sanctioned load, and electricity duty or tax set by the state. Many boards also add a fuel or power purchase adjustment that changes from time to time.

Rooftop solar and the PM Surya Ghar subsidy

The central subsidy for home rooftop solar has made small systems much more affordable. A 3 kW system, enough for many families, now costs well under what it did a few years ago once the subsidy is credited. The catch is the paperwork and the rules: you need panels with domestic content (DCR), an empanelled vendor and an application through the national portal.

Why the same units cost different amounts

It surprises people that an electricity bill in India can differ so much between neighbouring states. There are three main reasons. First, each state commission sets its own slab limits and rates, based on what its distribution companies spend on buying and supplying power. Second, states choose very different ways to support households: Tamil Nadu gives free units in every cycle, Delhi and Karnataka run subsidy schemes, and others keep rates low through cross-subsidy from commercial users. Third, taxes and fixed charges vary, from a small duty in some states to a sizeable one in Maharashtra.

The practical lesson is to ignore national averages and work from your own state's table. The calculators on this site do exactly that, and each page explains the local quirks that change the final number.

Reading the meter yourself

Most homes now have a digital meter that shows a running total in kWh. Note it on the same day each week. The difference between two readings is the units you used. Doing this for a month or two shows you your normal daily use, which makes any sudden rise easy to spot and gives you time to act before the bill arrives. Many boards are also rolling out smart prepaid meters, which show your balance and usage in an app.

Power cuts and inverter batteries

In many towns, a home inverter with a tubular battery is still essential. Choosing the right battery size saves money twice: you buy less battery, and you waste fewer units charging it. Small habits that lower any Indian bill

  • Read your meter halfway through the billing cycle and compare it with the slab limits for your state.
  • Run the AC at 24 to 26 degrees with a ceiling fan. In most rooms it feels just as cool and uses far less.
  • Switch geysers off after use, or fit a timer. A geyser left on all day is one of the biggest hidden loads.
  • Replace old fans with BLDC models. They use roughly half the power and pay back quickly in hot states.
  • If you are close to a subsidy limit, such as 200 units in Delhi or your Gruha Jyothi entitlement in Karnataka, plan the last week of the cycle.
