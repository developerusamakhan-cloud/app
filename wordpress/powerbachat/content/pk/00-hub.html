How an electricity bill in Pakistan is worked out

The idea is simple, even if the bill does not make it look that way. Your meter records how many units (kilowatt-hours) you used in the billing month. Those units are split into slabs. The first 100 units are charged at one rate, the next 100 at a higher rate, and so on. Add the slabs together and you have the energy charge. Then the government adds taxes, and the regulator adds or removes a fuel adjustment depending on what fuel actually cost a few months earlier.

The 200 unit line matters more than anything else

Households that stay at 200 units or less every month for six months in a row are called protected consumers, and they pay a fraction of the normal rate. Cross 200 units even once and the cheaper rates are gone for the next six months. In practice that one extra unit can add thousands of rupees to the bill, which is why so many families switch off the AC in the last week of the billing cycle.

Small habits that make a real difference

You do not need to give up comfort to pay less. Most of the savings we see come from a handful of habits:

  • Read your own meter around day 20 of the billing cycle. If you are close to the next slab, you still have time to ease off.
  • Set the AC to 26 degrees with a fan on, instead of 18 degrees with the fan off. It feels much the same and uses far less.
  • Replace the last few old tube lights and halogen bulbs with LEDs. They cost little and pay back within a season.
  • Run the water pump and the iron in the morning, when the house load is low anyway.
  • Keep an eye on the fridge seal. A loose door gasket can quietly add units every single day.
