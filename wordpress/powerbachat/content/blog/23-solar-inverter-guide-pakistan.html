Panels get most of the attention, but the solar inverter is the part that decides how your system actually behaves. It turns the DC power from the panels into AC for your home, decides whether you can run during load-shedding, and talks to the grid if you export power. It is also the part most likely to fail first. This guide explains the types, how to size one, and what to check on the spec sheet before you hand over the money.

The three types of inverter

On-grid (grid-tied)

An on-grid inverter works only when the grid is present. It feeds solar power into your home and sends any extra to the grid through a bidirectional meter. It has no batteries, so it is the cheapest option per kW and the most efficient. The catch: when the grid goes off, it shuts down for safety, even in bright sunshine. If your area has little load-shedding and your goal is a lower bill, this is usually the best value.

Hybrid

A hybrid inverter connects to panels, batteries and the grid at once. In daytime it runs the house from solar, charges the batteries and can export surplus if allowed. During load-shedding it switches to batteries. For most Pakistani homes that face both high bills and outages, a hybrid inverter is the practical middle ground. Note that some cheaper hybrid models cannot export to the grid, or cannot run without a battery, so ask before you buy.

Off-grid

An off-grid inverter runs from panels and batteries and treats the grid (or a generator) only as a backup charger. It cannot export power. It suits places with very weak or no grid supply, farms and tube wells, but it needs a larger battery bank and gives you no bill credit for surplus.

Sizing: inverter kW vs panel kW

The inverter rating is the AC power it can deliver. The panel rating is the DC power your array produces in ideal conditions. They do not have to match exactly.

  • Panels slightly larger than the inverter is normal. Panels rarely reach their full rating in Pakistani heat, so many installers put roughly 10 to 30 percent more panel kW than inverter kW. Check the inverter's maximum PV input before going higher.
  • Inverter large enough for your peak load. If you run two 1.5 ton ACs, a fridge, fans and a water pump at the same time, add up those watts. The inverter must handle that, plus the starting surge of motors.
  • Grid export limits. For a grid connection, the size you can install is usually tied to your sanctioned load. Check with your distribution company first.

MPPT: what the numbers mean

MPPT stands for maximum power point tracking. It is the circuit that keeps panels working at their best voltage as sunlight and temperature change. On a spec sheet, look at three things.

  • Number of MPPT inputs: two trackers let you put panels on different roof faces or angles without one string dragging down the other.
  • MPPT voltage range: your panel string voltage must sit inside this window in both cold winter mornings and hot summer afternoons. Your installer should do this calculation, not guess.
  • Maximum input current per tracker: large modern panels have high current. If the tracker limit is lower, you lose power.

Older inverters with PWM charge controllers are cheaper but waste a noticeable share of panel output. For any new system, insist on MPPT.

Battery voltage

Battery voltage is the DC voltage of the battery bank the inverter is built for. Small units often use 24V, most home hybrid inverters use 48V, and some larger ones use high-voltage battery packs. The battery bank must match. Higher voltage means lower current for the same power, which means thinner cables, less heat and better efficiency.

Also check which battery types the inverter supports. Lithium batteries need an inverter that can talk to the battery's management system, or at least has a proper lithium charging profile. Heat, dust and IP rating

Inverters lose output and age faster when hot. Summer in Pakistan is hard on electronics, so placement matters as much as the model.

  • Mount it in shade with space around it for airflow, never in direct afternoon sun or a closed cupboard.
  • The IP rating tells you how well it resists dust and water. An IP65 unit is designed for outdoor use; a lower-rated one should stay indoors.
  • Check the derating temperature on the spec sheet. Many inverters reduce output above a certain temperature, which can easily be reached on a rooftop in June.
  • Clean fan vents and filters every few months, especially in dusty cities.

Warranty and after-sales

A panel warranty may run for decades, but an inverter warranty is typically much shorter, often around five years, sometimes extendable. Before buying, ask:

  1. Is the warranty from the manufacturer or only from the shop?
  2. Is there an authorised service centre in your city?
  3. Will a replacement be given while yours is being repaired?
  4. Does the warranty cover lightning and voltage surges, or exclude them?

A well known brand with local support is often worth paying a little more for. A cheap solar inverter with no service network can leave you without power for weeks.

Net metering approval

If you want to export power, your inverter must be acceptable to your distribution company. In general, that means a grid-tied or hybrid model that meets the required standards, installed by a vendor approved for this work, with an application filed with your distribution company and the meter replaced. The rules and buyback terms have been revised in recent years and may change again, so check the latest position on the NEPRA website and with your distribution company before you buy.

A worked example

A family in Faisalabad uses around 600 units a month in summer, has a 5 kW sanctioned load and gets two to four hours of load-shedding a day. Their peak load is one 1.5 ton inverter AC, a fridge, six fans, lights and a small pump, which adds up to roughly 3 to 3.5 kW.

An on-grid unit would cut their bill but leave them in the dark during outages, so they choose a 6 kW hybrid inverter at 48V. They install about 7 kW of panels on two roof faces, using both MPPT inputs. A lithium battery covers fans, lights, the fridge and internet through the evening outage, while the AC runs mostly in daytime on solar. The inverter sits in a shaded, ventilated corner of the stairwell, not on the roof. For price ranges, see our 5kW solar system price page.

Common mistakes

  • Buying on kW rating alone without checking MPPT range, maximum PV input and surge capacity.
  • Choosing a 24V inverter to save money, then struggling with cable heat and limited battery options.
  • Mounting the inverter in direct sun on the roof.
  • Assuming every hybrid inverter can export or run without a battery.
  • Skipping proper earthing and surge protection.
  • Not asking whether the model is accepted for net metering in your area.

Frequently asked questions

What size solar inverter do I need for a 5 kW system?

Usually a 5 kW inverter, or a 6 kW hybrid if your peak load is high. Panels can be somewhat larger than the inverter, within its PV input limit.

Can a hybrid inverter work without batteries?

Some can, many cannot. Check the spec sheet or ask the dealer for a written answer.

Is an on-grid inverter useful during load-shedding?

No. It shuts down when the grid fails, for the safety of line workers.

How long does an inverter last?

A good one in a cool, clean spot often lasts around ten years, but it will usually need a repair or replacement before your panels do.
