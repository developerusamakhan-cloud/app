After weeks or months of negotiation, you agree on a number. Then the insurer sends a document, often called a "Release of All Claims," and asks you to sign and return it. This is the most important page in the whole claim. Once it is signed and the money is paid, the claim is over, usually forever, even if your injury turns out to be worse than anyone thought.

This guide walks through a **car accident settlement agreement** clause by clause, so you know what you are signing and what to ask about. It is general information, not legal advice. For a large settlement, having an attorney read the release is worth the cost.

## What a release does

In exchange for payment, you give up your right to make any further claim against the people and companies named in the release, for the injuries and losses it describes. Most releases are written broadly on purpose, because the insurer wants certainty that the claim is closed.

That is normal. The question is whether the release says what you agreed to, and nothing more.

## 10 clauses to check

### 1. The amount and what it covers

The number should match exactly what you agreed. Check whether it covers bodily injury only, property damage only, or both. If property damage was settled separately, make sure this release does not cover it again in a way that cuts off anything still owed.

### 2. Who is being released

Usually the at-fault driver, the car's owner and their insurer. Watch for wording that releases "all other persons, firms or corporations." That can include people you did not intend to release, such as another driver who shared the fault or your **own insurer** on an underinsured motorist claim.

### 3. Your own underinsured motorist claim

If your damages are larger than the at-fault driver's policy, you may have a claim under your own underinsured motorist coverage. Many policies require your insurer's consent before you settle with the at-fault driver. Get that consent in writing first. Our guide on [claims worth more than the policy limits](/car-accident-claim-more-than-policy-limits/) explains why this matters.

### 4. Known and unknown injuries

Most releases cover "all claims, known and unknown, foreseen and unforeseen." This means you cannot come back later if you need surgery you did not expect. It is the main reason not to settle an injury claim until you have finished treatment or reached maximum medical improvement.

### 5. Liens and who pays them

Many releases make you responsible for paying all medical liens from the settlement, and some ask you to **indemnify** the insurer, meaning repay it if a lienholder later comes after it. Know the lien amounts before you sign. If Medicare paid any bills, there are specific rules for repaying it. Our guide on [attorney fees and medical liens](/attorney-fees-and-medical-liens/) explains how liens reduce your check.

### 6. Confidentiality

Some releases ask you to keep the settlement amount private. Ask what that means in practice, for example whether you can tell family or a tax professional. It is often negotiable in ordinary car accident claims.

### 7. No admission of fault

Almost every release says the payment is not an admission of liability. This is standard and does not affect your payment.

### 8. Tax language

How the payment is described can matter. Language saying the money is paid on account of personal physical injuries supports tax-free treatment under federal law. Our guide on [whether a car accident settlement is taxable](/is-car-accident-settlement-taxable/) explains the rules.

### 9. Payment terms

Check when payment will be made, how, and to whom. If you have a lawyer, the check usually goes to the lawyer's trust account. If there is a lienholder, it may be named on the check. Ask for a date.

### 10. Other people's claims

If your spouse may have a claim for loss of companionship, or a child was injured, the release may need their signatures or, for a minor, court approval in many states. Make sure the release covers only the claims it should.

## Warning signs: stop and ask

- The amount or names do not match what you agreed
- It releases "all persons" and you have another possible claim
- You have not finished medical treatment
- You do not know your lien amounts
- You have an underinsured motorist claim and have not told your insurer
- The release covers property damage you have not been paid for
- You are being pushed to sign the same day

You can ask for changes. Insurers revise releases more often than people expect, especially to correct names, remove unrelated parties or narrow confidentiality.

## What happens after you sign

You return the signed release, often notarized. The insurer then issues payment, commonly within a few weeks, although timing varies by state and insurer. If you have a lawyer, they deposit the check, pay liens and costs, take their fee and send you the rest along with a settlement statement showing every deduction. Ask for that statement and check it line by line. Our [take-home calculator](/settlement-calculator-take-home/) shows what to expect.

## A simple pre-signing checklist

1. Is the amount right, and does it say what it covers?
2. Are only the right people released?
3. Did I get my own insurer's consent if I have a UIM claim?
4. Have I finished treatment?
5. Do I know every lien amount?
6. Am I comfortable with the confidentiality terms?
7. Does the payment description support tax-free treatment?
8. When and how will I be paid?

If you are still deciding whether to accept the number itself, go back to our guide on [the first settlement offer](/should-you-accept-the-first-settlement-offer/), which covers the questions to ask before you get to this stage.

[cf_note type="warning"]A release is a contract. Once signed and paid, it is very hard to undo, even if you later learn your injury is worse. If anything is unclear, ask in writing for a plain-language explanation or have an attorney review it.[/cf_note]
