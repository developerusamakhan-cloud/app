This electricity bill calculator works for every household in Pakistan, whichever company supplies you. Because all distribution companies, from LESCO to K-Electric, charge homes on the same national slab tariff, one calculator can give you a reliable base estimate anywhere in the country. Choose your company above, enter your units and you will see the full breakdown.

Below the calculator we have also answered the questions people search for most: what a 100 unit bill costs, what 200 or 300 units come to, and where the bill starts to hurt. If you already know your company, you may prefer its own page, such as the LESCO bill calculator or the K-Electric bill calculator, which add local advice.

How the calculator works

The tool takes your units for the month and splits them into slabs. Each slab is charged at its own rate. It then adds electricity duty and 18 percent GST on top. If you tick "Protected consumer", it uses the much lower rates that apply to homes that have stayed at 200 units or less for six months in a row. The result is the base bill before the monthly fuel adjustment and any arrears.

What each reading usually means in real life

Numbers are easier to judge when you can picture the house behind them. These are typical patterns we see, not rules:

  • 100 units. A small home or flat with lights, two or three fans, a fridge and a TV, in a mild month. Very achievable in winter.
  • 200 units. The same home in a warmer month, or a family of five with a washing machine and water pump. The protected line.
  • 300 units. A typical home that runs an air cooler through summer or an AC for a few hours on the hottest nights.
  • 500 units. One inverter AC used most nights from May to September, plus normal household use.
  • 800 to 1,000 units. Two or three ACs, a large fridge and freezer, a water motor and a geyser. This is where solar usually pays back fastest.

How many units your appliances use

If you are not sure where your units go, this rough monthly guide helps. Multiply the power by the hours of use and the days in the month, then divide by 1,000 to get units.

  • Ceiling fan (about 80 watts, 12 hours a day): around 29 units a month. A DC inverter fan uses roughly half.
  • LED bulb (12 watts, 6 hours a day): about 2 units a month each.
  • Fridge: usually 40 to 70 units a month, more for older models and deep freezers.
  • 1.5 ton inverter AC used 8 hours a night: roughly 200 to 300 units a month, depending on heat and thermostat setting.
  • Water pump (0.75 kW, one hour a day): about 22 units a month.
  • Iron (1,000 watts, half an hour a day): about 15 units a month.
  • Electric geyser (2,000 watts, three hours a day): about 180 units a month.
  • LED TV (80 watts, five hours a day): about 12 units a month.

Add up the appliances you use and you will usually land close to your meter reading. Our guide on what one unit of electricity is explains the maths with more examples.

Why the average price per unit rises

Look at the last column of the tables. As usage goes up, the price of each unit on average goes up too. That is because each extra block of units is charged at a higher rate than the block before. A home using 600 units does not pay three times what a 200 unit home pays. It pays a good deal more than that. The practical lesson: cutting your top few dozen units saves far more than cutting the same amount from the bottom of the bill.

The fuel adjustment, explained in one paragraph

Every month the regulator compares what fuel was expected to cost when the tariff was set with what it actually cost a couple of months earlier. The difference is passed on as a per-unit charge or refund, printed on your bill as FPA or FCA. It can be positive or negative, and it changes every month. That is the main reason any calculator, ours included, cannot predict a bill to the last rupee. Our full fuel price adjustment guide explains how to read it.

Five quick ways to use this calculator

  1. Mid-month check. Read your meter on day 15, double it and see where you are heading.
  2. Before buying an AC. Add about 250 to 300 units to your summer reading for a 1.5 ton inverter AC used most nights, and see the new bill.
  3. Checking a bill. Enter the units printed on your bill and compare the energy charge.
  4. Solar decisions. The bill at your summer units is roughly what solar could save. Our solar calculator takes it from there.
  5. Family discussions. Showing the slab breakdown is often more persuasive than asking everyone to switch things off.

When your bill and the calculator disagree

Start with the units. If they match your meter, the difference will almost always be the fuel adjustment, arrears from a previous bill, or a late payment surcharge. If the units look too high, photograph the meter with the date visible and raise it with your company's local office before the due date. Bills based on estimated readings are the most common source of genuine errors.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a 200 unit electricity bill in Pakistan?

It depends on whether you are protected. The tables above show both cases, with taxes included. A protected home pays a fraction of what a non-protected home pays for the same 200 units.

How much is a 300 unit bill?

Check the 300 unit row in the first table. Every 300 unit bill is non-protected, because protected status ends above 200 units.

Does this electricity bill calculator work for all companies?

Yes. LESCO, IESCO, MEPCO, FESCO, GEPCO, PESCO, HESCO, SEPCO, QESCO, TESCO and K-Electric all use the same national household tariff.
