If you have ever stared at your bill wondering how they arrived at that number, you are not alone. This guide explains how electricity bill is calculated in India, step by step, using a real example. Once you see the pattern, you can check any bill in a couple of minutes and spot errors before you pay.

Step 1: units from the meter

Everything starts with units. A unit is one kilowatt-hour, the energy a 1,000 watt appliance uses in an hour. Your meter keeps a running total. The bill takes the reading at the end of the billing period, subtracts the reading at the start, and the difference is your units consumed.

Many boards now include a photo of the meter on the bill or in the app, which makes this check even easier. Check this first every time. If the present reading on your bill is higher than your meter shows, you have been over-billed. If the bill says the reading was average, assessed or estimated, expect a catch-up later.

Step 2: splitting units into slabs

Indian domestic tariffs are built in slabs. The first block of units is cheapest, and each further block costs more. A bill of 350 units in a state with slabs of 0 to 100, 101 to 300 and above 300 is split into 100 units, 200 units and 50 units.

In most states the slabs are telescopic: each block is charged at its own rate. A few states use non-telescopic billing above a limit, where all units are charged at the rate of the slab your total falls in.

Step 4: the fixed charge

Most states add a fixed or demand charge every month, whatever you use. It is usually based on your sanctioned load in kilowatts. A 2 kW home pays twice what a 1 kW home pays. This is why a very small user can still see a noticeable bill, and why it is worth checking that your sanctioned load is not higher than you need.

Step 5: duty and taxes

The state government adds electricity duty or tax, usually as a percentage of the energy charge, and sometimes of the fixed charge as well. The rate varies widely between states, from a few percent to over fifteen percent. That difference alone can change a bill noticeably.

Step 6: adjustments

Many boards add a variable charge that reflects the changing cost of buying power. It goes by different names: fuel adjustment charge (FAC) in Maharashtra, power purchase adjustment (PPAC) in Delhi, fuel and power purchase cost adjustment (FPPCA) elsewhere. It is usually a per-unit amount or a percentage, and it changes from time to time. It is the main reason two bills with the same units can differ.

Step 7: subsidies and credits

Several states reduce bills through schemes. Tamil Nadu gives the first 100 units free in every two-month cycle. Delhi subsidises bills up to certain limits. Karnataka's Gruha Jyothi waives the energy charge up to each household's entitlement. These appear as credits or free units on the bill. If you expected a subsidy and it is missing, check that you are registered or have opted in.

Step 8: arrears, surcharges and the final amount

Finally, any unpaid amount from earlier bills is added as arrears, and paying after the due date adds a late payment surcharge or interest. Some bills also show a security deposit adjustment or meter rent. The total payable is everything added together.

A worked example, start to finish

Let us walk through a Maharashtra bill for 350 units on a single-phase connection.

  1. Units: 350.
  2. Slabs: 100 units in the first slab, 200 in the second, 50 in the third.
  3. Energy charge: each block times its rate, including wheeling, added together.
  4. Fixed charge: the monthly single-phase amount.
  5. Duty: a percentage of the energy and fixed charges.
  6. FAC: whatever per-unit adjustment is in force that month.

Why bills jump in summer

When usage rises, two things happen at once. You use more units, and more of those units fall into expensive slabs. So a bill can double when usage rises by less than half. Understanding how electricity bill is calculated makes this obvious, and it explains why cutting the last few dozen units of a summer month saves so much more than cutting the same amount in winter.

Monthly or bi-monthly?

Before you check any slab, find out how often your board bills you. Tamil Nadu and Kerala bill most homes every two months, while Delhi, Maharashtra and Uttar Pradesh bill monthly. How electricity bill is calculated for a two-month cycle depends on the state: Tamil Nadu sets its slab limits for the full 60 days, while Kerala sets them monthly and applies them to each month of the cycle. Mixing these up is the most common reason a home calculation does not match the bill.

Common mistakes on bills

  • Estimated readings followed by large catch-ups.
  • Wrong sanctioned load, which inflates the fixed charge every month.
  • Payments not credited, which show up as arrears.
  • Missing subsidy when you are eligible but not registered.

For each, keep evidence: a dated photo of the meter, payment receipts and a copy of your registration. Raise complaints through your board's app or office before the due date.

Frequently asked questions

How is electricity bill calculated per unit?

Units are split into slabs and each slab is charged at its own rate. Fixed charges, duty and adjustments are then added, so your effective rate per unit is higher than the first slab rate.

What is a fixed charge on an electricity bill?

A monthly charge based on your sanctioned load, payable whatever you use.

Why do two bills with the same units differ?

Usually because of the fuel or power purchase adjustment, a tariff revision, arrears or a subsidy change.

Where can I calculate my own bill?

Use the calculator at the top of this page or our electricity bill unit rate calculator.

Can I reduce the fixed charge?

Sometimes. The fixed charge depends on your sanctioned load. If it is higher than you need, ask your board about reducing it, but keep it at or above your real peak load.
