With gas pressure low in many Dhaka kitchens and LPG prices moving every month, a lot of families are asking whether an induction cooker would be cheaper. The honest answer is: it depends on your electricity slab, your cylinder price and how much you cook. This guide shows how to work it out for your own home, with rough figures you can adjust.

The three options in short

  • Piped gas: many older connections in Dhaka and some other cities are billed at a fixed monthly rate for a single or double burner, whatever you cook. New household connections have been very hard to get for years in most areas, and supply pressure is often weak at peak cooking times.
  • LPG cylinder: the most common option for new flats. The regulator, BERC, announces the price of a standard 12 kg cylinder each month, although shops do not always sell at that price.
  • Induction: an electric hob that heats the pan directly using a magnetic field. It is fast, clean and adds units to your electricity bill.

If you have a working piped gas line with decent pressure and a fixed monthly charge, it is hard to beat on cost, because extra cooking costs you nothing. The real comparison for most families is LPG against induction.

How many units does induction cooking use?

A typical induction hob is rated at around 1.8 to 2.2 kilowatts at full power, but you rarely use full power for long. Boiling water uses high power for a few minutes; simmering a curry uses a low setting for longer. Some rough units per meal figures for a family of four:

  • Tea for four: about 0.1 unit.
  • A pot of rice: about 0.3 to 0.5 unit.
  • A fish or chicken curry: about 0.4 to 0.7 unit.
  • Dal or vegetables: about 0.2 to 0.4 unit.

A household that cooks two full meals and a breakfast at home often ends up using around 2 to 3 units a day on induction, or roughly 60 to 90 units a month. Slow cooking like beef bhuna or haleem pushes that up; a rice cooker for rice pulls it down a little, since it is also electric but well insulated. If the idea of a unit is unclear, see what one unit of electricity is.

How to compare with an LPG cylinder

The simplest method is to compare a cylinder with the electricity that would replace it.

  1. Know how long a cylinder lasts you. Many families of four get about 3 to 5 weeks from a 12 kg LPG cylinder.
  2. Convert it to units. A gas burner wastes a lot of heat around the pan, while induction puts most of its energy into the pan. As a rough rule, the cooking you do with one 12 kg cylinder takes about 70 to 90 units on induction.
  3. Price those units at your top slab. This is the step people skip. Induction units are added on top of your fans, fridge and lights, so they are charged at the highest slab you reach that month, not your average rate.
  4. Compare with the cylinder price you actually pay, including delivery.

The slab effect

The same 80 units cost very different amounts depending on where they land. If your home uses 150 units without induction, adding 80 takes you to about 230, still in the middle slabs. If you already use 350 units because of an AC in summer, 80 more takes you past 400 units, into the most expensive slab, and those cooking units cost much more each.

So induction is often clearly cheaper than LPG in winter, when the rest of the house uses little, and closer to even, or sometimes dearer, in peak summer months for homes with an AC. Some families switch: induction for most of the year, the LPG cylinder for heavy cooking in the hottest months. You can test both cases in the electricity bill calculator.

Worked example: a family of four in Uttara

Nasrin's family in Uttara uses one 12 kg LPG cylinder a month. Assume for this example that it costs them around Tk 1,400 delivered; check the current BERC price and your local shop for the real figure.

Winter: the house uses about 180 units a month without cooking. Switching fully to induction adds about 80 units, taking them to 260. Using the table, the bill difference between 180 and 260 units is the monthly cost of induction. At rough mid slab rates, that typically comes to well under the cylinder price, so induction wins clearly.

Summer: with the AC running, the house already uses about 380 units. Adding 80 units takes them to 460, and most of those cooking units now fall above 400. Compare the bill at 380 and 460 units in the table. The gap is much larger than in winter, and it can come close to the cost of the cylinder, wiping out most of the saving.

Their decision: induction for daily cooking all year, but in the hottest months they keep the cylinder for long cooking like biryani and cut AC hours a little to stay near 400 units. Their cylinder now lasts about three months instead of one.

Wiring and load

A 2 kilowatt induction cooker draws around 9 amps. That is fine on a proper 15 amp power socket with good wiring, but it is too much for a thin extension board or a light duty 5 amp socket. Warm plugs, a burning smell or a socket that browns are warning signs to stop and call an electrician.

Also check your sanctioned load. If you run an induction hob, an AC, a water pump and an iron at the same time, a small connection may trip. Your sanctioned load is printed on the bill; our guide on how to read your electricity bill shows where. If you use a prepaid meter, watch the balance closely for the first month; the prepaid meter guide explains the deductions.

Cookware

Induction only works with magnetic cookware. Test your pans with a fridge magnet: if it sticks firmly to the base, the pan will work. Cast iron and most steel pans are fine. Plain aluminium pots, clay pots and many traditional patil and kadai will not work unless they are marked as induction compatible. A flat base matters too; a warped pan heats unevenly and wastes power. Budget for one or two good induction pans when you buy the hob.

Safety

Induction has no open flame and no gas leak risk, which is a real advantage in small, closed kitchens. The hob surface does get hot from the pan, so treat it as hot for a few minutes after cooking. Keep the air vents clear and do not place it on a soft surface. LPG, by contrast, needs a good regulator, a hose without cracks and a kitchen with ventilation. Check the hose regularly and turn off the cylinder valve at night.

So which should you choose?

  • Working piped gas at a fixed rate: keep it, and add an induction hob only as a backup for low pressure times.
  • LPG only and moderate electricity use: an induction cooker will usually save money.
  • High summer use near 400 units: use induction most of the year and keep LPG for the peak months.
  • Frequent load-shedding: keep a cylinder as backup, since induction stops when the power does.

Frequently asked questions

How many units does an induction cooker use per day?

A family of four cooking two or three meals at home usually uses around 2 to 3 units a day, or roughly 60 to 90 units a month. Long, slow cooking raises that figure.

Is induction always cheaper than an LPG cylinder?

No. It depends on which slab the extra units land in. In months when your home stays well below 400 units it is usually cheaper; in summer months with an AC the saving can shrink or disappear.

Can I use my existing pots on induction?

Only if a magnet sticks to the base. Steel and cast iron usually work, while aluminium and clay pots generally do not.

Can I plug an induction cooker into any socket?

Use a proper 15 amp socket on sound wiring, not an extension board. If the plug or socket gets warm, have an electrician check the circuit.
