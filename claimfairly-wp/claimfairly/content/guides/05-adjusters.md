An adjuster's job is to close your claim for a fair amount under the policy. Their employer's business depends on paying claims efficiently. Both things are true at once, and it explains a lot about how offers are built.

Here is what happens between the day you report the crash and the day you get a number.

## Step 1: Liability

Before anything else, the adjuster decides who was at fault and by how much. They review the police report, photos, statements and damage patterns. If they think you share the blame, your offer will be reduced, and in some states it can be eliminated. See [comparative vs contributory negligence](/comparative-vs-contributory-negligence/).

## Step 2: Economic damages

Next they add up losses with a paper trail:

- **Medical bills.** Often they look at what was actually paid, not the billed amount, and they check whether each treatment looks "reasonable and necessary" for the injury.
- **Lost wages**, usually proven with a letter from your employer or pay stubs.
- **Other costs** like prescriptions, medical equipment and mileage to appointments.

Expect them to question treatment that looks excessive, started late or went on longer than usual for the diagnosis.

## Step 3: Pain and suffering

This is the part with the most room for disagreement. Many large insurers use **claims evaluation software** that scores injuries using medical codes, treatment types and duration. The exact rules are private.

The results often land near the **multiplier method**: medical costs times a number between about 1.5 and 5. Our [pain and suffering calculator](/pain-and-suffering-calculator/) shows you that range.

Things that push the number up: objective injuries like fractures, surgery, injections, long treatment, permanent effects and consistent records. Things that push it down: gaps in treatment, soft tissue diagnoses only, prior injuries to the same area, and a long wait before the first doctor visit.

## Step 4: Limits and leverage

The final offer is shaped by practical limits:

- **Policy limits.** If their driver carries $25,000 per person, that is usually the ceiling.
- **Settlement authority.** Adjusters can often settle only up to a certain amount without a supervisor's approval.
- **Litigation risk.** A represented claimant with a strong case, or a lawsuit on file, changes the math, because defense costs money.

## Why the first offer is usually low

Opening low is a normal negotiating move, not a personal insult. Some people accept it, which closes the file quickly. Others push back, and the number moves. It is common for the final settlement to be meaningfully higher than the first offer, especially when the claimant has documentation.

## How to respond to a low offer

1. **Do not accept on the spot.** You can say you need time to review it.
2. **Ask how they got the number.** Which bills did they include? What did they allow for pain and suffering? Did they assign you any fault?
3. **Fill the gaps.** Send missing records, bills, wage proof and photos.
4. **Make a written counter** with a specific number and reasons. A [demand letter](/demand-letter-generator/) is the standard way to do it.
5. **Know your deadline.** Negotiations do not pause your state's statute of limitations.
