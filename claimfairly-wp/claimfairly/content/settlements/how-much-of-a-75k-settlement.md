A $75,000 settlement often involves a more serious injury, like a fracture, injections or a long course of therapy, and usually a well documented claim. At this level more cases involve a lawsuit being filed, which can raise the fee percentage.

The quick answer: with a one third attorney fee and nothing else taken out, you would keep about **$50,003**. Real settlements usually have case costs and medical liens too, so most people keep less. ## A realistic $75,000 example

Here is a typical breakdown with a one third (33.33%) fee taken from the full settlement, $3,500 in case costs, and a $9,000 medical lien that the lawyer negotiated down by 25%.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Settlement | $75,000 |
| Attorney fee (one third (33.33%)) | minus $24,998 |
| Case costs | minus $3,500 |
| Medical lien after reduction | minus $6,750 |
| **You keep** | **$39,753** |

If the fee were calculated **after** costs instead, you would keep about **$40,919**, a difference of $1,167. That is why it is worth reading your fee agreement closely. Our guide on [attorney fees and medical liens](/attorney-fees-and-medical-liens/) explains each deduction in detail.

## How the fee percentage changes a $75,000 settlement

Before costs and liens:

| Fee | Attorney fee | Left for you |
|---|---|---|
| 30% | $22,500 | $52,500 |
| 33.33% | $24,998 | $50,003 |
| 35% | $26,250 | $48,750 |
| 40% | $30,000 | $45,000 |

## What kind of case settles for about $75,000?

Claims around $75,000 often include medical bills of $15,000 to $30,000, specialist care such as injections or orthopedic follow up, a recovery of six months or more, and meaningful lost income. Fractures, injuries needing injections and cases with lasting symptoms are common at this level, especially when fault is clear. To see where your own claim may land, enter your bills, lost wages and injury severity in the [car accident settlement calculator](/car-accident-settlement-calculator/), which also applies your state's fault rule.

## Watch the fee tier

Many fee agreements use one third if the case settles before a lawsuit and 40% after. On $75,000, that difference is about $5,000. If your case is close to settling, it is fair to ask whether filing is really necessary, and how the fee will be calculated if it is.

## How long a $75,000 settlement usually takes

Claims of this size often take nine to eighteen months, and a larger share involve a lawsuit being filed to move negotiations along. After you sign the release, payment usually arrives within two to six weeks. Liens can add time. Read [how long a car accident settlement takes](/how-long-does-a-car-accident-settlement-take/) for the full timeline.

## Is $75,000 a fair number for your claim?

Run through these before you accept:

- Does it cover all medical bills, including future care your doctor recommended?
- Does it cover lost wages and out-of-pocket costs?
- Is there a reasonable amount left for pain and suffering? Check with the [pain and suffering calculator](/pain-and-suffering-calculator/).
- Did the insurer assign you any fault, and do you agree with it?
- Is it close to the other driver's policy limit?
- After fees and liens, is the amount you keep enough to cover what the crash cost you?

If several answers are "no," read [should you accept the first settlement offer](/should-you-accept-the-first-settlement-offer/) before you respond.

## How to keep more of your $75,000 settlement

- Ask whether your fee is calculated before or after costs.
- Ask your lawyer to negotiate every medical lien.
- Ask for an itemized list of costs with receipts.
- Request a written settlement statement before you sign the release.
- Check whether your own MedPay coverage already paid some bills without a lien.

## Three versions of the same $75,000 settlement

| Scenario | Fee | Costs | Lien paid | You keep |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No lawyer, lien paid in full | $0 | $0 | $9,000 | $66,000 |
| Lawyer, settled before a lawsuit (33.33%) | $24,998 | $3,500 | $6,750 | $39,753 |
| Lawyer, settled after a lawsuit (40%) | $30,000 | $7,000 | $6,750 | $31,250 |

This comparison assumes the same $75,000 gross settlement in all three cases, which is not always realistic. Represented claimants often settle for more, and lawyers usually negotiate liens down. The point is to see how each factor moves your share, not to suggest which path is right for you.

## When the money arrives

With $75,000 or more, it is worth pausing before big purchases. Paying off high interest debt and rebuilding savings often does more good than it looks like on paper.

Before you sign a $75,000 release, four checks matter most:

1. **Make sure every week of missed work is in the demand**, including paid time off you had to use.
2. **Ask whether any lien is from Medicaid or Medicare.** Government liens follow their own rules and must be resolved before the money is paid out.
3. **Keep the lost wage paperwork** with your settlement statement. It helps if anyone later asks what the money was for.
4. **Ask your doctor about future care before you sign.** Once the release is signed, the claim cannot be reopened for treatment you need later.

## Lost wages: the part of a $75,000 claim people undercount

Lost income is often the second biggest piece of a $75,000 claim, after medical bills. Insurers pay for what you can prove, so the paperwork matters.

| If you are | What usually proves the loss |
|---|---|
| An hourly or salaried employee | A letter from your employer with dates missed, pay rate and hours, plus recent pay stubs |
| Someone who used sick days or PTO | The same employer letter, showing the paid leave you had to use |
| Self-employed or a gig worker | Tax returns for the last two years, invoices, 1099s, bank deposits, and canceled jobs |
| Someone who lost a promotion or overtime | Records of the overtime or raise you would have had, in writing if possible |

**A simple example:** 12 weeks off work at $1,000 a week is **$12,000** in lost wages. Doctor's notes that say you could not work during those weeks connect the time off to the crash.

**On taxes:** under federal law, money received because of a physical injury is generally not taxable, and that usually includes the lost wage part. Interest and punitive damages are exceptions. See our guide on [whether a settlement is taxable](/is-car-accident-settlement-taxable/).
