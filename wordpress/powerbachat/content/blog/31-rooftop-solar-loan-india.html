The biggest hurdle to going solar is rarely the roof. It is the upfront cost. Even after the central subsidy, a family has to find a large sum before the panels start saving anything. A rooftop solar loan spreads that cost into monthly payments, and if the numbers are right, the EMI can be close to what you save on the bill. This guide explains how bank finance works alongside PM Surya Ghar, how to compare the EMI with your saving, and where people go wrong.

Why loans became part of PM Surya Ghar

When the PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana was launched, the government knew many households could not pay the full system price upfront and then wait for the subsidy to arrive. So public sector banks were brought in to offer finance through the same process. You can apply for the subsidy and a loan from the national portal, pick a registered vendor, and the bank funds a large part of the cost.

How the bank loan works in general

The details depend on the bank and change from time to time, but the broad pattern has been fairly consistent:

  • Collateral-free for small systems. For smaller home systems, roughly up to 3 kW, public sector banks have offered loans without asking you to pledge property. Larger systems may need extra security or a different loan product.
  • Interest linked to the repo rate. The rate is usually set as a spread over the RBI repo rate, so it can move when the repo rate moves. Ask the bank whether your rate is fixed or floating.
  • Margin money from you. The bank does not usually fund 100 percent. You pay a share yourself, often around 10 percent, called margin money.
  • Loan tenure of several years. Repayment periods have commonly been up to around ten years. A longer tenure lowers the EMI but raises the total interest.
  • Basic eligibility. You need a grid connection in your name, your own roof, a working income and a reasonable credit history.

Because these terms are set by banks and can be revised, treat the points above as a general guide. Check the current options on the PM Surya Ghar national portal and confirm the final rate, fees and tenure with your bank branch before you sign.

What happens to the subsidy

This is the part many people miss. The central subsidy is paid after the system is installed, inspected and the net meter is fitted. It is not paid upfront. When you take a loan, the bank usually lends against the full project cost minus your margin money. Once the subsidy lands in your account, you can choose to pay it into the loan to cut the balance, which is usually the smart move.

EMI versus bill saving: the only test that matters

A loan for solar makes sense when the monthly saving on your bill is close to, or more than, the EMI. To compare the two honestly:

  1. Work out your generation. In most of India, one kW of panels makes roughly 3.5 to 4.5 units a day on average, so a 3 kW system makes around 300 to 400 units a month.
  2. Work out what those units are worth. Solar first replaces your most expensive units, the ones in the top slab. Our electricity bill unit rate calculator shows your effective rate.
  3. Check how your discom handles export. Under net metering, units you send to the grid are set off against units you import. Rules on credit and settlement differ by state.
  4. Compare with the EMI after the subsidy is paid in. The EMI that matters is the one on the reduced balance, not the starting one.

A worked example

Take a family in Pune using about 350 units a month. They choose a 3 kW system. These figures are rough and only for illustration.

  • System price: roughly ₹1.8 lakh to ₹2 lakh from a registered vendor.
  • Margin money: about ₹20,000 paid by the family.
  • Loan amount: about ₹1.6 lakh to ₹1.8 lakh.
  • Subsidy: see the table above for the current figure for 3 kW. Suppose they pay it straight into the loan a few months later.
  • Remaining balance: roughly ₹1 lakh or a little more, over about seven to ten years.

At an interest rate in the region of 7 to 8 percent, a balance of that size works out to an EMI of very roughly ₹1,200 to ₹1,600 a month, depending on tenure. Their 3 kW system makes about 330 units a month, which covers most of their use. Their bill drops from a few thousand rupees in summer to little more than fixed charges. In this case the bill saving comfortably beats the EMI, and once the loan ends, the saving is all theirs.

Now take a family using only 120 units a month. Their saving per unit is lower because they sit in cheap slabs, and a 3 kW system would export most of its power. For them, a smaller system, or no rooftop solar loan at all, may make more sense. Run your own numbers through the PM Surya Ghar calculator before deciding.

Common pitfalls

  • Oversizing the system. A bigger system means a bigger loan. If your discom pays little for exported units, extra panels add EMI without adding much saving.
  • Not paying the subsidy into the loan. If you spend the subsidy elsewhere, you keep paying interest on money you did not need to borrow.
  • Ignoring the floating rate. If the repo rate goes up, your EMI or tenure may increase. Read the sanction letter carefully.
  • Using an unregistered vendor. Only vendors registered on the portal qualify for the subsidy. A cheaper quote from outside the scheme can cost you the subsidy entirely.
  • Hidden fees. Ask about processing charges, insurance bundled with the loan and prepayment penalties. Many banks do not charge for prepayment, but confirm it.
  • Weak panels or inverters. A loan runs for years. Cheap equipment that fails in year four leaves you paying EMI on a system that is not saving. Our guide to DCR vs non-DCR solar panels explains which panels the scheme requires.

Is a solar loan better than paying cash?

If you have the money sitting in a savings account earning less than the loan rate, paying cash is usually cheaper. If paying cash would empty your emergency fund, a loan is the safer choice. Many families do a mix: pay a larger margin from savings, borrow less, and clear the loan early using the subsidy.

Also think about how long you will stay in the house. Solar panels last 25 years, but if you plan to move in three years, a long loan may not suit you.

Steps to apply

  1. Register on the national portal with your discom consumer number.
  2. Get a feasibility approval from your discom.
  3. Choose a registered vendor and get a quote for the size you need.
  4. Apply for the loan through the portal or directly with a participating bank.
  5. Pay your margin money, let the vendor install, and apply for net metering.
  6. After inspection, the subsidy is released. Pay it into the loan.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need collateral for a rooftop solar loan?

For small home systems under PM Surya Ghar, public sector banks have generally offered collateral-free loans. Larger systems or different loan products may need security, so confirm with your bank.

What interest rate will I pay?

Rates are usually linked to the RBI repo rate and vary by bank and system size. Check the current rate on the portal and with the bank before applying.

Can I prepay the loan with the subsidy?

Usually yes, and it is often the best use of the subsidy. Ask whether any prepayment charge applies.

Will my EMI be more than my bill saving?

It depends on your usage, system size and tariff. Families with high monthly use often save more than the EMI; very low users may not.
