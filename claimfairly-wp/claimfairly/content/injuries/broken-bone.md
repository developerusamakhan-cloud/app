A broken bone is one of the clearest injuries to prove after a car accident. It shows up on an X-ray, it has a well understood treatment and it comes with obvious limits on daily life: a cast, crutches, weeks without driving or working. That is why fracture claims are often valued higher than soft tissue claims with similar medical bills.

Even so, fracture claims vary enormously. A broken finger that heals in a splint and a shattered leg that needs plates and screws are both "broken bones," but they lead to very different settlements. This page explains what drives the value, shows example ranges, and covers the evidence that helps most.

## Estimate your broken bone claim

The calculator is preset to **serious**, the usual category for fractures. Pick **moderate** for a simple fracture that healed quickly without surgery, or **severe** if you have lasting limits or permanent hardware.

## What affects the value of a fracture claim

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Type of fracture | A simple break heals more predictably than a compound (open) or comminuted (shattered) fracture |
| Which bone | Leg, hip, pelvis and spine fractures affect walking and work more than a finger or toe |
| Surgery | Plates, screws or rods usually mean higher bills, longer recovery and a higher multiplier |
| Healing time | Many fractures take six weeks or more to heal, with therapy afterward |
| Lasting effects | Arthritis, reduced motion, a limp or hardware that needs removal later add value |
| Your job | A broken wrist affects a carpenter's income more than an office worker's |

## Common fractures in car accidents

- **Wrist and forearm**, often from bracing against the steering wheel or dashboard
- **Collarbone (clavicle)**, often from the seat belt or side impact
- **Ribs**, from the seat belt, airbag or steering wheel
- **Leg, ankle and foot**, from the footwell collapsing in a frontal crash
- **Hip and pelvis**, in more severe collisions
- **Spine (vertebrae)**, including compression fractures

## The recovery timeline matters

Recovery from a fracture often includes a cast or splint, follow up X-rays, sometimes surgery, and physical therapy to rebuild strength and range of motion. Keep a record of each stage: the weeks you could not drive, work, sleep comfortably or care for your family are central to pain and suffering. Many people also need help at home, which can be part of the claim.

## Example broken bone claims

| Claim | Medical bills | Lost wages | Severity | Estimated range |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Broken wrist, cast, no surgery | $7,000 | $2,500 | Moderate | $23,500 to $30,500 |
| Broken ankle with surgery | $32,000 | $9,000 | Serious | $137,000 to $169,000 |
| Fractured femur, surgery, lasting limp | $85,000 | $30,000 | Severe | $455,000 to $540,000 |

These assume no shared fault. The larger examples show why policy limits matter: few drivers carry enough insurance to pay a claim like the femur example, so other coverage, such as your own underinsured motorist policy, becomes important.

## Future medical costs

Ask your doctor whether you may need hardware removal, more therapy, or treatment for post-traumatic arthritis later. Future costs belong in your claim, and they are easy to forget if you settle too early. Add them in the "future medical costs" field in the calculator above, and ask your doctor to put the recommendation in writing.

## Policy limits often cap fracture claims

A fracture with surgery can easily exceed a minimum policy like $25,000 per person. If the at-fault driver's limit is low, look at your own **underinsured motorist coverage**, and ask whether any other policies apply, such as an umbrella policy or a commercial policy if a work vehicle was involved. Enter the limit in the calculator to see the practical ceiling.

## Evidence checklist for a fracture claim

1. ER records and the first X-ray report
2. Orthopedic follow up notes and any surgical report
3. Physical therapy records showing progress and limits
4. Photos of the cast, brace, surgical scars and equipment
5. Receipts for crutches, braces, home help and transport
6. Employer letter about missed work or duty changes
7. A doctor's note about future care or permanent effects
8. The police report and vehicle photos

## Fractures in no-fault states

In many no-fault states, a fracture meets the serious injury threshold that allows a pain and suffering claim against the at-fault driver. New York's definition, for example, specifically lists fractures. Read [at-fault vs no-fault states](/at-fault-vs-no-fault-states/) and check your state on our [state rules pages](/states/).

## Costs people forget with fractures

Fractures create costs well beyond the hospital bill. Keep receipts for all of these:

- Crutches, a walker, a wheelchair rental or a knee scooter
- Shower chairs, grab bars and other home equipment
- Rides to appointments if you could not drive, including rideshare trips
- Help with childcare, cleaning, cooking or yard work
- Clothing that fits over a cast or brace
- Parking and mileage for follow up and therapy visits

These are usually small individually, but over a few months they can add up to hundreds or thousands of dollars.

## Scarring, hardware and lasting effects

Surgical scars, visible hardware and a lasting change in how you walk or use your hand can all increase the pain and suffering part of a claim. Photograph scars as they heal, and ask your surgeon whether the hardware may need to be removed later and whether you are at higher risk of arthritis in that joint. If you have permanent limits, such as reduced range of motion, ask your doctor to document them with measurements.

## Returning to work and driving

Many people cannot drive while wearing a cast on a leg or arm, and some jobs are impossible with a fracture. Ask your doctor for written guidance on when you can drive and return to work. Lost wages include time off for surgery, follow up appointments and therapy, not only the first weeks after the crash.
