For most Pakistani families, the summer electricity bill is the biggest household expense of the year after rent and school fees. From May to August, fans, coolers and ACs run for long hours, units climb into the expensive slabs, and the bill can triple. The way to beat it is not one trick but a plan that starts before the heat arrives. Here is a month-by-month approach that works for most homes.

April: prepare before the heat

  • Service every AC. Clean the filters and coils, check the gas and make sure the outdoor unit has shade and space. A clean AC cools faster and runs less.
  • Replace the worst fans. If you are replacing fans this year, do it now. Efficient DC fans use roughly half the power of old ones.
  • Check the water tank and pump. Fix leaking float valves before the summer rush.
  • Look at your history. Take a photo of the twelve-month table on your bill. Note how many units you used last June and July.

If you are a protected consumer, April is also when to decide how hard you will fight to keep the status. Our guide to the protected consumer rule explains what is at stake.

May: the first heatwave

May is when many families lose protected status and when the first big jump in the bill arrives. Read your meter every week. If you are close to 200 units and protected, the choices you make in the last ten days of the billing month matter most. If you are not protected, aim to stay in the lower slabs as long as possible. Use coolers during the dry heat of May, when they work best, and keep ACs for the nights.

June: the long hot days

  • Set ACs to 26 degrees with a fan. It is the single biggest saving most homes can make.
  • Cool one room well. Bring the family together in one cooled room in the hottest afternoon hours.
  • Block the sun. Close curtains on west-facing windows by midday. A light-coloured roof coating helps top-floor rooms.
  • Move chores to the morning. Laundry, ironing and pumping water before noon keeps the evening load down.

July and August: humidity and monsoon

When the monsoon arrives, coolers lose much of their effect because the air is already humid. This is when ACs work hardest and bills peak. Use the AC's dry mode on muggy evenings that are not extremely hot; it removes moisture with less cooling. Keep doors closed and check that the AC drains properly. If your bill is still climbing, read the meter weekly and see where you stand against the slabs with the electricity bill calculator.

Every week: the two-minute meter check

The habit that makes the biggest difference is simple: read the meter on the same day every week and write the number down. Subtract last week's reading to get your weekly use, multiply by four and a bit for the month, and compare it with the slab limits. When you see trouble coming in week two, there is still time to act. When you see it on the bill, there is not.

What a summer electricity bill plan saves

Take a family in Faisalabad that used 650 units last July. This year they serviced both ACs in April, replaced three fans, set the ACs to 26 degrees, moved laundry and the water pump to the morning and closed the west-facing curtains every afternoon. Their July usage came in near 520 units. Because the units they saved were the most expensive ones at the top of the bill, their summer electricity bill fell by much more than the 20 percent drop in units. Check the effect for your own numbers with the FESCO bill calculator or the one for your company.

Load-shedding and the summer bill

Summer is also when load-shedding is most common in many areas. A UPS keeps fans and lights running, but every recharge comes from your meter. An old battery or an inefficient UPS can add 30 to 50 units a month in summer, right when every unit is expensive. If your UPS battery is several years old and backup has fallen, replacing it may lower the bill as well as restore backup. Our battery backup calculator shows how much battery you really need, and the guide to lithium vs tubular batteries compares the options.

Guests, Eid and weddings

Summer often brings guests, Eid gatherings and weddings, with more people, more cooking and late nights. A single busy week can add a large number of units. If you know a big event is coming, plan around it: keep the rest of the month lighter, cool only the rooms in use and switch off ACs in empty rooms. For protected consumers, one festive week is often what pushes the month past 200 units.

Bigger steps for next summer

  • Replace the oldest AC with an inverter model. Our guide on inverter vs non-inverter ACs explains the saving.
  • Consider rooftop solar. Summer is exactly when solar produces the most. See the 5kW solar system price page.
  • Insulate the roof if you live on the top floor.

None of these steps needs to happen all at once. Pick one each summer, and within a few years the house runs on far fewer units for the same comfort.

Common summer mistakes

  • Running the AC at 18 degrees with the fan off.
  • Leaving the cooler running in humid weather when it adds moisture rather than comfort.
  • Ignoring a pump that runs for hours because of a leaking tank.
  • Checking the bill only when it arrives, when it is too late to change anything.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my summer electricity bill so high?

Higher usage pushes more units into expensive slabs, and the fuel adjustment can add to it. Cooling is usually the main cause.

What AC temperature saves the most?

26 degrees with a ceiling fan is a good balance of comfort and cost for most homes.

Do coolers save electricity?

Yes, in dry heat. They use a fraction of an AC's power. In humid monsoon weather they are much less effective.

Is it worth replacing fans before summer?

For fans that run most of the day, yes. Efficient DC fans use roughly half the power of old ones and pay back within a season or two.

Does a white roof really help?

Yes, for top-floor rooms. A light-coloured, reflective roof coating keeps the room below noticeably cooler, so the AC and fans work less.

How often should I read my meter?

Once a week is enough to stay ahead of the slabs.
