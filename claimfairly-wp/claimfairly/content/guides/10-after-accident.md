Right after a crash, your heart is pounding and it is hard to think clearly. That is normal. This checklist is here so you do not have to remember everything in the moment. Save it on your phone or print it for your glove box now, before you ever need it.

It is organized by time: the first minutes at the scene, the first few days, and the weeks of the insurance claim. Each step protects either your health, your claim, or both.

## At the scene

1. **Check for injuries and call 911** if anyone is hurt. Your health comes first, always. Do not move someone with neck or back pain unless there is immediate danger like fire.
2. **Get to safety.** If the cars can move and it is safe, pull out of traffic. Turn on your hazard lights and set out flares or triangles if you have them.
3. **Call the police**, even for a minor crash if your state or the situation calls for it. A police report is one of the most useful documents in any claim, because it records the scene, the drivers and often an initial view of fault.
4. **Exchange information** with the other driver: name, phone, address, driver's license number, plate number, insurance company and policy number. A photo of their license and insurance card is the fastest way.
5. **Take photos** of both cars from several angles, the damage up close, the road, traffic signs and signals, skid marks, debris and weather. Take more than you think you need.
6. **Get witness names and numbers.** People leave quickly, and independent witnesses carry a lot of weight when fault is disputed.
7. **Do not admit fault** or apologize. Stick to facts. Fault is decided later, with all the evidence, and an apology at the scene can be used against you.
8. **Note the details:** time, location, direction of travel, speed limit and what you remember. A voice memo works well.

## What to collect: a quick list

| Item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Police report number | Official record of the crash |
| Other driver's insurance and plate | Needed to file your claim |
| Photos and video | Shows damage, positions and conditions |
| Witness contacts | Supports your version of events |
| Names of officers | Helps you get the report later |
| Medical records from day one | Links injuries to the crash |

## Within 24 to 72 hours

1. **See a doctor**, even if you feel fine. Adrenaline hides pain, and some injuries like whiplash and concussions show up a day or two later. A visit soon after the crash also links your injuries to the accident, which matters for the claim. Read about [whiplash claims](/injuries/whiplash-car-accident-settlement/) and [concussion claims](/injuries/concussion-car-accident-settlement/) if you have symptoms.
2. **Report the crash to your insurer.** Most policies require prompt notice, even if you were not at fault.
3. **Report to the other driver's insurer** and get a claim number in writing. Our guides to how [State Farm](/insurers/how-state-farm-handles-car-accident-claims/), [GEICO](/insurers/how-geico-handles-car-accident-claims/) and [Progressive](/insurers/how-progressive-handles-car-accident-claims/) handle claims explain what to expect.
4. **Start a folder** (paper or digital) for everything: the police report number, photos, bills, receipts, and a log of calls with dates and names.
5. **Write down what happened** while it is fresh, in your own words.
6. **Check your own coverage.** Look for MedPay or PIP, rental coverage, collision and uninsured or underinsured motorist coverage on your declarations page.

## During the claim

- **Follow your treatment plan.** Gaps in treatment are one of the most common reasons adjusters cut injury claims.
- **Keep every receipt**, including prescriptions, parking and mileage to appointments.
- **Track missed work** with dates and hours, and ask your employer for a letter.
- **Keep a short pain journal.** A few lines a day about pain, sleep and what you could not do. It becomes powerful evidence later.
- **Be careful with recorded statements** to the other driver's insurer. You can politely decline or ask to answer questions in writing.
- **Stay off social media** about the crash, your injuries or your activities. Posts can be taken out of context.
- **Do not sign a release** or cash a check marked "final" until you know the full cost of your injuries.
- **Get your car assessed** for [diminished value](/what-is-diminished-value/) once it is repaired. It is money many people never ask for.

## Understand your state's rules

Your state decides who pays first and what happens if you share some of the blame:

- In **no-fault states**, your own PIP pays medical bills first. See [at-fault vs no-fault states](/at-fault-vs-no-fault-states/).
- In **contributory negligence states**, even 1% fault can bar your claim. See [comparative vs contributory negligence](/comparative-vs-contributory-negligence/).
- Every state has a **filing deadline**. Find yours on our [state rules pages](/states/).

## When to call a lawyer

Consider a free consultation if any of these apply:

- You were seriously hurt, hospitalized or still in treatment
- Fault is disputed or the other driver blames you
- The other driver is uninsured or has low limits
- The insurer is delaying, denying or pressuring you
- A filing deadline is getting close
- A commercial vehicle, rideshare or government vehicle was involved

## If you were a passenger

Passengers follow most of the same steps: get medical care, collect information from every driver involved, take photos and get the police report. Passengers are rarely at fault, so their claims are often simpler. You may be able to claim against the at-fault driver's insurance, and in some cases against both drivers' insurers. Your own auto policy's MedPay or PIP may also cover your medical bills, even when you were riding in someone else's car.

## If a rideshare, delivery or work vehicle was involved

Commercial crashes add layers. Rideshare and delivery companies often carry different coverage depending on whether the driver was logged in, carrying a passenger, or off duty. Company vehicles may be covered by a business policy. Collect the company name, the vehicle number and the driver's employer, take screenshots of the app trip if you were a passenger, and report the crash to the company as well as the insurers. These claims are often worth a free consultation with a lawyer.
