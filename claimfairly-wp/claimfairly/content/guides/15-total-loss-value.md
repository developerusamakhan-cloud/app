A total loss offer arrives as a single number, often with a short note that it was "based on market data." Behind that number sits a valuation report several pages long, and that report is where low offers come from. The good news is that it is built from inputs you can check: the cars it compared yours to, the options it gave your car credit for, and the adjustments it made for condition and mileage.

This guide explains how **total loss car value** is calculated, the errors that show up most often, and how to push back with evidence rather than frustration. If you are still at the stage of learning whether your car is totaled at all, start with our guide on [what happens when your car is totaled](/what-happens-when-your-car-is-totaled/).

## Where the number comes from

Most large insurers do not value cars by hand. They hire valuation companies, such as CCC, Mitchell or Audatex, that produce a report for each total loss. The basic method is the same everywhere:

1. Find **comparable vehicles**: the same year, make and model, ideally the same trim, sold or listed near you recently.
2. **Adjust** each comparable's price for differences from your car: mileage, options, and condition.
3. Average or weight the adjusted prices into a single **actual cash value**.
4. Apply a **condition adjustment** for your car's state before the crash.
5. Add taxes and fees if your state requires it, and subtract your deductible on a first-party claim.

Every step involves a judgment call. Each judgment call is a place where the number can be too low.

## Ask for the full report first

Before you argue about the number, ask the adjuster in writing for the **complete valuation report**, including the list of comparable vehicles with their VINs or listing details, mileage, options, location and price, and every adjustment applied. Many states' claim rules, often based on an NAIC model regulation, expect the insurer to explain how a cash total loss settlement was calculated. Without the report you are negotiating blind.

## The seven most common errors

### 1. The wrong trim or package

A base model listed in place of your higher trim can cut thousands from the value. Check the trim name and the factory packages against your window sticker or build sheet. A VIN decoder from the manufacturer or a dealer can confirm what the car left the factory with.

### 2. Missing options

Navigation, a sunroof, upgraded wheels, towing packages and safety tech all add value. If the report lists them as missing, the comparables are being adjusted down against your car.

### 3. Comparables that are not comparable

Look at each comparable's trim, mileage, distance from you and date. Cars that are hundreds of miles away, from a cheaper trim, or from a time when prices were different are weak comparisons.

### 4. Mileage adjustments that are too aggressive

If your car had lower miles than the comparables, its value should be adjusted up. If it had higher miles, down. Check that the direction and size of each mileage adjustment makes sense.

### 5. Condition deductions

Reports often rate condition and subtract for wear. If your car was well kept, show it: service records, recent photos, a clean interior. A "typical" or "fair" rating on a car that was in excellent shape costs money.

### 6. Recent work and new parts ignored

New tires, brakes, a new battery or a major service shortly before the crash may support a higher value. Bring receipts. Not every insurer adds full value for maintenance, but documented recent work is a fair argument.

### 7. Taxes and fees left off

In states that require it, the settlement should include sales tax and title and registration fees for a replacement car. Check whether they were included.

## Build your own valuation

Gather your own evidence in an afternoon:

1. **Search current listings** within a reasonable distance for the same year, make, model and trim with similar miles. Save screenshots with the price, mileage, options and date.
2. **Check online value guides** like Kelley Blue Book, Edmunds and J.D. Power, using your exact trim, options, mileage and condition. Print the results.
3. **List your car's options and recent work** with receipts.
4. **Calculate an average** of your best comparables, adjusted for any mileage differences.

## A worked example

Insurer's offer: **$14,000**, based on three comparables averaging $14,400 with a "fair" condition deduction.

Your findings:

| Problem in the report | Effect |
|---|---|
| Listed as base trim, car was the sport trim | About $1,200 too low |
| Missing the technology package | About $700 too low |
| One comparable 300 miles away, older listing | Pulls the average down |
| Condition rated "fair" despite service records | $400 deduction |
| Sales tax not included | Missing entirely |

Your counter, with three local sport-trim listings averaging $16,200 and service records: **$16,200 plus tax and fees**. A middle number of $15,600 plus tax would still be $1,600 more than the first offer, and that is a common kind of result when the errors are documented.

## How to send the counter

Put it in writing. A short letter or email works:

- The claim number
- The specific errors you found in their report
- Your comparables, attached
- Your requested value and the total with tax and fees
- A polite request for a revised offer by a specific date

## If you still cannot agree

**Appraisal clause.** Many auto policies have one for disputes over the amount of a loss on your own coverage. You and the insurer each hire an appraiser. If they disagree, they choose an umpire, and a decision by any two is binding on the amount. You pay your appraiser and share the umpire's cost, so this is worth it mainly when the gap is a few thousand dollars or more.

**State insurance department.** If the insurer will not explain its valuation or will not respond, a complaint can prompt a review.

**Third-party claims.** If the other driver's insurer is paying, the appraisal clause in your own policy does not apply to them. You can negotiate, claim under your own collision coverage instead and let your insurer pursue them, or in the end sue the at-fault driver, often in small claims court for smaller amounts.

## Don't forget the rest

A total loss offer covers the car itself. On a claim against the at-fault driver, also ask for rental or loss of use, towing and storage, and other out-of-pocket costs. If you were hurt, the injury claim is separate and should usually wait until treatment ends.
