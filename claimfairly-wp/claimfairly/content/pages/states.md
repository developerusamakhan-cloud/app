The same crash can be worth very different amounts depending on where it happened. A driver who is 20% at fault gets 80% of their damages in California, but can get nothing at all in Virginia. That difference comes from state law, not from the insurer.

This page puts every state's basic rules side by side. Click a state name to open its full page, where the claim calculator is already set to that state's rules.

## The three rules that matter most

- **Fault system.** In at-fault states the other driver's insurer pays for your injuries. In no-fault states your own PIP coverage pays first, and suing for pain and suffering has extra hurdles.
- **Negligence rule.** This decides what happens when both drivers share blame. It ranges from pure comparative (you can always recover something) to contributory (any fault can bar you).
- **Filing deadline.** The statute of limitations for an injury lawsuit runs from 1 to 6 years depending on the state. Miss it and the claim is usually over.

## What the negligence rules mean in dollars

Say your claim is worth $50,000 and you are found 30% at fault:

| Rule | What you could recover |
|---|---|
| Pure comparative | $35,000 |
| Modified, 50% bar | $35,000 (but $0 at 50% fault or more) |
| Modified, 51% bar | $35,000 (but $0 above 50% fault) |
| Contributory | $0 |

## No-fault and choice no-fault states

Twelve states change who pays first after a crash. In **no-fault states** (Florida, Hawaii, Kansas, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, New York, North Dakota and Utah), your own personal injury protection (PIP) pays your medical bills and some lost wages no matter who caused the crash. In **choice states** (Kentucky, New Jersey and Pennsylvania), your policy option decides whether you can claim pain and suffering for a minor injury. Read [at-fault vs no-fault states](/at-fault-vs-no-fault-states/) for the details.

## Filing deadlines at a glance

| Deadline for an injury lawsuit | Examples |
|---|---|
| 1 year | Tennessee |
| 2 years | California, Texas, Florida, Georgia, Pennsylvania, Illinois, Ohio, Virginia |
| 3 years | New York, North Carolina, Michigan, Maryland, Massachusetts, Washington |
| 4 to 6 years | Nebraska, Utah and Wyoming (4), Missouri (5), Maine, Minnesota and North Dakota (6) |

These are the general deadlines for injury lawsuits after a car accident. Property damage claims, claims against a government vehicle and claims involving a minor can have different deadlines. The insurance claim itself should be reported much sooner, usually within days.

## Minimum insurance and why it matters

Every state except New Hampshire requires drivers to carry at least some liability insurance, and the minimums vary widely. Minimums are written as three numbers: for example, **25/50/25** means $25,000 per injured person, $50,000 per accident for injuries, and $25,000 for property damage. If the driver who hit you carries only the minimum, that may be the most their insurer will pay, no matter how serious your injuries are. That is why your own **underinsured motorist coverage** can matter so much.
