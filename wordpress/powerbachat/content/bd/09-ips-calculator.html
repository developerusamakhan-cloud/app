How to use the IPS calculator

  1. Set how many fans, lights and other appliances you will actually switch on during a cut.
  2. Add anything not listed, in watts. The wattage is printed on the appliance or its adapter.
  3. Choose your battery type. Tubular batteries are the common IPS choice; lithium batteries give more backup per battery.
  4. Set the number of batteries. Most home IPS units use one or two 12 V batteries.

The maths, simply

Battery energy is voltage times amp-hours. A 12 V 130 Ah tubular battery holds 1,560 watt-hours when full. To protect its life, you should use only about half, around 780 watt-hours. The IPS loses some energy converting it, so about 85 percent reaches your appliances, around 660 watt-hours. With two fans and a few lights using 200 watts, that is a little over three hours of backup.

Lithium batteries can safely use around 90 percent of their capacity, which is why a smaller lithium battery can match a larger tubular one.

IPS size: VA versus watts

The IPS rating, in VA, decides how much load you can run at once. The battery decides how long it runs. The IPS calculator suggests a minimum size with about 30 percent headroom, because fans and motors draw extra power when they start. If you plan to run a fridge or water pump, choose a larger IPS. Typical setups

  • Small flat: a 600 to 700 VA IPS with one tubular battery for two or three fans and lights, giving a few hours of backup.
  • Family home: a 1,000 to 1,100 VA IPS with one or two batteries for four or five fans, lights, a TV and a router.
  • Long or frequent cuts: a solar-ready IPS or hybrid solar system with a lithium battery.

Matching backup to your area

Size for the load-shedding you actually get. If cuts usually last an hour or two, a single battery that covers two hours with margin is enough. If cuts come back to back in the evening, the battery needs time to recharge between them, and a lithium battery's faster charging becomes valuable. Watching your area's pattern for a couple of weeks before buying helps avoid both overspending and running flat.

Stretch your backup

  • Use energy-efficient DC fans. They use roughly half the power of old fans, almost doubling backup time.
  • Put only LED lights on the IPS circuit.
  • Keep irons, heaters, kettles and rice cookers off the IPS. They drain the battery in minutes.
  • Run fans at medium speed during cuts.

Battery care

Tubular batteries need distilled water topping up and should be kept in a cool, ventilated place. Avoid draining them completely on a regular basis. Replace old batteries in a bank together, not one at a time. When backup time falls noticeably, the battery is usually wearing out; check current prices on our battery price page before replacing it.

A worked example

A family in Rajshahi faces two cuts most evenings, each about an hour and a half, with an hour of power in between. They want to run three fans, five LED bulbs and the router. That is around 320 watts. The IPS calculator shows that one 12 V 130 Ah tubular battery gives about two hours, which covers one cut but may not recharge enough for the second. Two batteries give about four hours, enough for both cuts even with a partial recharge. They also switch two old fans to efficient DC fans, which cuts the load to about 240 watts and stretches the same pair of batteries well past five hours.

Reading the IPS display

Most IPS units show whether they are on grid or battery, the battery charge level and any fault codes. Learn what yours shows when the battery is full and when it is low. If the unit frequently shows overload, you are running more than it can handle; remove a load or choose a larger unit. If the battery drops to low far sooner than the IPS calculator suggests, the battery is likely worn.

IPS costs units too

Every time power returns, the IPS recharges the battery from the grid. Some energy is lost, and old batteries lose more. A typical home IPS can add 20 to 50 units a month, which you can price with our electricity bill calculator. Efficient fans and lights reduce both the backup load and the recharging units.

When solar makes more sense

If outages are long and frequent, a solar system with batteries charges the battery during the day for free, and can lower your bill. It costs more upfront, but for heavy users and areas with long cuts it often pays back. Read our IPS or solar guide to compare.

Before you buy

Once the IPS calculator gives you a size and battery, check current prices on our IPS price page, ask the dealer for a sine wave unit with local service, and insist on recent battery stock with a warranty card. A good setup, sized correctly, will give years of reliable backup.

Frequently asked questions

How long will a 130 Ah IPS battery last?

With about 200 watts of fans and lights, a single 12 V 130 Ah tubular battery gives a little over three hours. Use the IPS calculator with your own load for a precise figure.

What size IPS do I need?

Add up the watts you will run at once and allow about 30 percent extra. The calculator suggests a minimum size.

Is lithium better for an IPS?

If your IPS supports it, lithium gives more usable backup per battery and lasts much longer.

Why is my IPS backup getting shorter?

Batteries lose capacity with age, heat and deep discharge. A tubular battery several years old may need replacing.

Can I add a second battery to my IPS?

Only if the IPS supports the higher battery voltage or parallel connection. Check the manual, and replace batteries in a bank together.

Does the IPS calculator work for solar batteries?

Yes. The same maths applies to any battery bank and inverter, whether it charges from the grid or from solar panels.
