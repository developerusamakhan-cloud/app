If you ask an insurance adjuster how they got their diminished value offer, there is a good chance the answer is "17c." It sounds official. It is not a law, though, and it was not built from resale data. Once you see how it works, it is easy to understand why the numbers come out so low.

## Where 17c came from

In 2001 the Georgia Supreme Court decided *State Farm v. Mabry*, a case about whether insurers had to pay diminished value on first-party claims. As part of the settlement process that followed, a formula for estimating diminished value was referenced in paragraph 17c of a court document. Insurers adopted it, first in Georgia and then far beyond it.

That origin matters. The formula was a practical way to process a flood of claims, not a careful study of what accident history does to used car prices.

## The 17c formula, step by step

**Diminished value = market value x 10% x damage multiplier x mileage multiplier**

### Step 1: Market value

The car's value right before the accident. Insurers often use NADA (now J.D. Power) values.

### Step 2: The 10% cap

Take 10% of that value. This is the base loss, and it is the most 17c will ever pay. On a $40,000 car, the ceiling is $4,000, no matter how bad the damage was.

### Step 3: Damage multiplier

| Damage | Multiplier |
|---|---|
| Severe structural damage | 1.00 |
| Major structural and panel damage | 0.75 |
| Moderate structural and panel damage | 0.50 |
| Minor structural and panel damage | 0.25 |
| No structural damage, cosmetic only | 0.00 |

### Step 4: Mileage multiplier

| Miles | Multiplier |
|---|---|
| 0 to 19,999 | 1.0 |
| 20,000 to 39,999 | 0.8 |
| 40,000 to 59,999 | 0.6 |
| 60,000 to 79,999 | 0.4 |
| 80,000 to 99,999 | 0.2 |
| 100,000 or more | 0 |

## Example: a $30,000 car

Moderate structural damage, 35,000 miles:

- $30,000 x 10% = $3,000
- $3,000 x 0.50 = $1,500
- $1,500 x 0.80 = **$1,200**

## Three reasons 17c undervalues your car

### 1. The 10% cap is arbitrary

Nothing in the formula looks at real sales. For newer or higher end vehicles with structural repairs, dealers often knock off more than 10% when they see the accident on a history report.

### 2. Damage multipliers cut deep

Moderate structural damage, which is a serious repair, already cuts the base loss in half. Cosmetic damage gets zero, even though a history report does not always say how minor the damage was.

### 3. Mileage wipes it out

Every 20,000 miles removes a big chunk, and at 100,000 miles the result is zero. Plenty of trucks and SUVs with 100,000+ miles sell for tens of thousands of dollars, and an accident record still lowers that price.

## How to push back on a 17c offer

- **Ask for the math.** Get the market value, damage multiplier and mileage multiplier they used. Mistakes are common, especially in the damage category.
- **Check the damage category** against your repair invoice. Frame or unibody work is structural.
- **Show real market evidence:** dealer quotes, trade-in offers that mention the accident, or listings of comparable cars with and without accident history.
- **Consider an independent appraisal** for a newer or valuable car.
- **Put it in writing** with a [demand letter](/demand-letter-generator/).

## Is 17c ever fair?

For an older, high mileage car with light damage, 17c may be close to what buyers would actually knock off. It is least fair for newer cars, low mileage cars and serious repairs, which is exactly where the money is. Use it as a floor, not the final word.
