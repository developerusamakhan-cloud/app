After months of waiting, the settlement check finally arrives, and a new worry shows up: is a car accident settlement taxable, and will a chunk of it go to taxes? For most people with a car accident injury claim, the answer is reassuring. Under federal law, money received because of a **physical injury** is generally not taxable income. But "generally" is doing some work in that sentence. Some parts of a settlement can be taxed, and how the agreement describes the money matters.

This guide explains the federal rules in plain English. It is general information, not tax advice. For your own situation, especially with a large settlement, talk to a tax professional.

## The basic rule

Section 104(a)(2) of the Internal Revenue Code excludes from income "damages (other than punitive damages) received ... on account of personal physical injuries or physical sickness." The IRS explains this in Publication 4345 and Publication 525.

In plain terms: if you were physically hurt in a car accident and the settlement compensates you for that injury, the money for that injury is generally **not taxable**, whether you received it as a lump sum or in payments.

## What is usually not taxable

| Part of the settlement | Usually taxable? |
|---|---|
| Medical expenses for the physical injury | No, unless you deducted them in earlier years |
| Pain and suffering from the physical injury | No |
| Lost wages caused by the physical injury | No |
| Emotional distress that flows from the physical injury | No |
| Property damage up to what the car was worth to you | No, it is a reimbursement |

The lost wages point surprises many people. Wages you earn at work are taxable. Wages paid to you in a physical injury settlement generally are not, because the payment is "on account of" the injury.

## What can be taxable

### Punitive damages

Money meant to punish the other driver, rather than to compensate you, is taxable, even in a physical injury case. Punitive damages are rare in ordinary car accident settlements, but they do appear in cases involving drunk driving or extreme recklessness. (There is a narrow exception for certain wrongful death cases in states where only punitive damages are available.)

### Interest

If the settlement or judgment includes interest, for example because the case went to court and interest accrued on a judgment, the interest is taxable.

### Medical expenses you already deducted

If you itemized and deducted medical expenses on an earlier tax return, and the settlement later reimburses those same expenses, the reimbursed amount may need to be included in income, up to the tax benefit you received. Most people take the standard deduction and never deducted the bills, so this rarely applies.

### Emotional distress without a physical injury

If the claim was only for emotional distress, with no physical injury, the money is generally taxable, except for the amount you spent on medical care for that distress.

### Property damage above your basis

Payment for a damaged car is usually a reimbursement, not income. In the unusual case that you received more than your adjusted basis in the car (roughly what you paid, minus certain adjustments), the excess could be a taxable gain. For most cars, which lose value over time, this does not happen.

## Why the wording of the settlement matters

The IRS looks at what the payment was for. A settlement agreement that clearly says the money is paid on account of personal physical injuries supports tax-free treatment. A vague agreement, or one that labels part of the money as something else, can cause questions later.

Before signing, check how the agreement describes the payment. If your settlement includes several types of damages, especially punitive damages or interest, ask your attorney or a tax professional how it should be allocated.

## A worked example

Lena settled her injury claim for $60,000 after a crash that caused a broken wrist. The agreement says the payment is for personal physical injuries.

- $15,000 covers medical bills she never deducted
- $10,000 covers lost wages
- $35,000 covers pain and suffering

**Federal tax: generally $0.** All three parts are on account of her physical injury.

Now suppose the case went to trial and the judgment added $5,000 in punitive damages and $2,000 in interest. Those $7,000 would generally be taxable, while the $60,000 would stay tax free.

## Do you get a 1099?

Insurers generally do not issue a Form 1099 for damages paid on account of physical injuries. You may receive one for taxable parts like interest or punitive damages. If you receive a 1099 that you think is wrong, ask the payer about it and talk to a tax professional before you file.

## What about attorney fees?

In a physical injury case, the whole settlement is generally excluded from income, including the part paid to your lawyer, so you do not need to deduct the fee. Fees matter for your take-home amount rather than your taxes. ## State taxes

Most states follow the federal treatment for physical injury settlements, but state rules are separate. If you live in a state with an income tax, ask a tax professional whether anything differs.

## Public benefits and liens

Taxes are not the only thing that can reduce what you keep. Health insurers, Medicare, Medicaid and hospitals may have liens on the settlement for the bills they paid. Means-tested benefits like SSI or Medicaid can also be affected by a large lump sum. If you receive needs-based benefits, talk to an attorney before the money arrives, because there are planning options that are much harder to use afterward.

[cf_note type="info"]This page describes federal tax rules in general terms. Tax law has exceptions, and the facts of your case matter. A short meeting with a tax professional before signing a large settlement is worth the cost.[/cf_note]

## Quick checklist before you sign

- Does the agreement say the payment is for personal physical injuries?
- Are punitive damages or interest included, and if so, how much?
- Did you deduct any of the medical bills on an earlier return?
- Do you receive SSI, Medicaid or other needs-based benefits?
- Will you receive a 1099 for any part?
