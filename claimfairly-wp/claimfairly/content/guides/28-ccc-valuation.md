If your car was declared a total loss, there is a good chance the offer came from a **CCC valuation** report. CCC is one of the largest companies that insurers hire to value totaled cars, and its market valuation report is the document behind the number in your settlement letter. Most people never see it unless they ask. They should, because every line in it is a choice that can move your payout by hundreds or thousands of dollars.

This guide explains what a CCC valuation report contains, how to read each section, the errors that show up most often, and how to dispute a low number with evidence.

## What a CCC valuation report is

A CCC valuation report estimates your car's **actual cash value** just before the crash: what it would have cost to buy a car like yours, in your area, in the same condition. To do that, the report looks at comparable vehicles that were for sale or recently sold near you, adjusts each one for differences from your car, and combines them into a single value.

Insurers use it because it is fast and consistent. That does not make it automatically right. The report is only as good as the information entered about your car and the comparables it picked.

## Ask for the full report

Before you negotiate, ask the adjuster in writing for the **complete** valuation report, not just the summary page. Many states' claim handling rules, often based on an NAIC model regulation, expect insurers to explain how a cash total loss settlement was calculated. A complete report usually runs several pages and lists every comparable vehicle and every adjustment.

## How to read the report, section by section

### 1. Your vehicle

The first section describes your car: year, make, model, trim, engine, mileage and the options the report credits it with. Check every line against your window sticker, build sheet or a VIN decoder. A missing package or the wrong trim lowers the value before anything else happens.

### 2. Condition

The report rates the condition of parts of your car, such as the interior, paint, body, tires and glass. These ratings are compared with a "typical" baseline for a car of that age. If your car was in better shape than typical, you should see a positive adjustment. If it was rated below typical without evidence, that is a fair point to challenge. Recent photos, detailing receipts and service records help.

### 3. Comparable vehicles

This is the heart of the report. For each comparable you will see its year, trim, mileage, options, location, price and source. Check:

- Is it really the same trim and generation as your car?
- How far away is it? Local market prices matter most.
- How old is the listing or sale?
- Does its mileage and option list make sense next to yours?

### 4. Adjustments to each comparable

Each comparable's price is adjusted for differences in mileage, options and condition, so that it reflects a car like yours. Look at the direction and size of each adjustment. A comparable with fewer miles than your car should be adjusted down, and one with more miles should be adjusted up. Large or unexplained adjustments deserve a question.

Some reports also include an adjustment that lowers dealer asking prices to estimate what cars actually sell for. If you see a line like this, ask the adjuster how it was calculated and what data supports it for your area.

### 5. Base value and final settlement

The adjusted comparables are combined into a base value. Then the report applies your car's condition adjustment and, depending on your state and policy, adds sales tax and fees and subtracts your deductible. Check that taxes and fees appear if your state requires them.

## The errors that show up most often

| Problem | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Wrong trim or package | Your car listed as a lower trim, or missing options |
| Weak comparables | Different trim, far away, older listings |
| Mileage adjustments | Adjusted in the wrong direction or by an unusual amount |
| Condition ratings | Below typical with no photos or notes to support it |
| Missing taxes and fees | Not included where your state requires them |

## How to dispute a low CCC valuation

1. **Write down every error** you found, with the page and line.
2. **Gather your own comparables:** current listings within a reasonable distance for the same year, trim and similar miles. Save screenshots showing price, mileage, options and date.
3. **Collect proof of condition and options:** the window sticker, service records, receipts for recent tires or major work, and recent photos.
4. **Check value guides** such as Kelley Blue Book and Edmunds with your exact trim and options.
5. **Send a written counter** listing each error, your evidence and your requested value, with a date for a response. Our [demand letter generator](/demand-letter-generator/) has a property damage version you can adapt.

If you and your own insurer still cannot agree, many policies include an **appraisal clause** for disputes over the amount of a loss. Our guide on [how insurers value a totaled car](/total-loss-car-value/) explains how appraisal works and what to do when the other driver's insurer is paying.

## A short example

An insurer offers $15,800 based on a CCC report. The report lists the car as a base trim, although it was the mid trim with a technology package, and two of the five comparables are more than 150 miles away. The owner sends three local listings for the correct trim averaging $17,400, the window sticker and recent tire receipts. The insurer corrects the trim and replaces the distant comparables, and the revised report lands at $16,900 plus tax and fees. Results vary, but documented errors like these are exactly what a reviewer can fix.

## Before you accept

A total loss settlement usually ends the property part of your claim, and the title transfers to the insurer. Make sure the number is right first. If you are still learning how the whole process works, read [what happens when your car is totaled](/what-happens-when-your-car-is-totaled/).
