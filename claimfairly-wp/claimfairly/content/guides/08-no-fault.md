"No-fault" sounds like nobody is to blame. That is not what it means. Fault still matters in every state. What changes is **who pays your medical bills first**, and **when you can sue** the other driver for pain and suffering.

## At-fault states

Most states are at-fault (also called tort) states. The driver who caused the crash is responsible for the other people's losses, and their liability insurance pays.

After a crash in an at-fault state you can:

- File a claim with the **other driver's insurer** (third-party claim)
- File with **your own insurer** under coverages like collision or MedPay, and let them go after the other insurer
- **Sue the at-fault driver** for all your damages, including pain and suffering

## No-fault states

In a no-fault state, your own **personal injury protection (PIP)** coverage pays your medical bills and some lost wages, up to your PIP limit, no matter who caused the crash. It is designed to get bills paid quickly without a fight over fault.

The trade-off: you usually **cannot sue the other driver for pain and suffering** unless your injury crosses a legal threshold.

### The no-fault states

Florida, Hawaii, Kansas, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, New York, North Dakota and Utah are no-fault states.

Kentucky, New Jersey and Pennsylvania are **choice no-fault** states: you pick a no-fault option or a traditional tort option when you buy your policy.

### The threshold for stepping outside no-fault

Each state sets its own line. There are two types:

- **Verbal thresholds** describe the injury: death, significant disfigurement, a fracture, permanent loss of a body function, or similar. New York's definition of "serious injury" in Insurance Law Section 5102 is a well known example.
- **Monetary thresholds** kick in once your medical bills pass a set dollar amount.

Once you meet the threshold, you can make a claim against the at-fault driver for pain and suffering and for losses above your PIP coverage.

## Property damage is different

No-fault rules usually cover **injuries only**. Damage to your car is still handled based on fault in almost every state, through the other driver's property damage liability or your own collision coverage. Michigan has its own special rules for vehicle damage, so check if you are there.

## What this means for your settlement

| Question | At-fault state | No-fault state |
|---|---|---|
| Who pays my medical bills first? | Other driver's insurer (or your MedPay) | Your own PIP |
| Can I claim pain and suffering? | Yes | Only past the threshold |
| Does fault matter? | Yes, for everything | Yes, for claims past PIP and property damage |
