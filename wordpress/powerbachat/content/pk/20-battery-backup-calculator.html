How to use the battery backup calculator

  1. Set how many of each appliance you want to run during an outage. Be honest about what you will actually switch on.
  2. Add anything not listed in the "Anything else" box, in watts. The label on the appliance or its charger usually shows the wattage.
  3. Choose your battery type. Tubular lead-acid batteries are the common choice for UPS systems; lithium (LiFePO4) batteries are increasingly used with solar.
  4. Set the number of batteries in your bank.

The big number is your estimated backup time. The verdict underneath tells you whether that covers a typical two-hour cut.

How the calculation works

The maths is straightforward, and it helps to understand it so you can check any installer's claims:

  • Battery energy is voltage times amp-hours. A 12 V, 200 Ah battery holds 2,400 watt-hours when full.
  • Usable energy is less than that. Lead-acid batteries last much longer if you only use about half their capacity before recharging. Lithium batteries can safely use around 90 percent.
  • Inverter losses take a share. We assume the inverter delivers about 85 percent of what it draws from the battery.
  • Backup time is the usable energy, after losses, divided by your running load in watts.

For example, one 12 V 200 Ah tubular battery gives about 1,200 watt-hours usable, and roughly 1,020 after inverter losses. Running two fans, four LED bulbs, a tube light, a router and two phone chargers, around 260 watts in total, that is just under four hours of backup.

Typical home setups and what they cover

  • One 12 V tubular battery with a 1 kVA UPS. The classic setup in many homes. Good for two or three fans and lights through a two-hour cut.
  • Two tubular batteries with a 2 kVA UPS. Covers most of a medium home's fans and lights for longer cuts, or a small fridge for a short one.
  • A 24 V or 48 V lithium bank with a hybrid solar inverter. Runs the essentials through long outages and stores daytime solar for the evening.

Choosing the right battery

Tubular lead-acid batteries are cheaper to buy and widely available. They need distilled water topping up, give off some gas while charging, and usually last a few years with good care. They should not be drained deeply on a regular basis.

Lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) batteries cost more up front, but they can be discharged much more deeply, recharge faster, need no maintenance and last many more cycles. For homes with daily load-shedding or solar systems that cycle the battery every day, lithium often works out cheaper over its life.

Getting the inverter size right

The inverter must handle the total load you switch on at the same time, plus a margin for motors and fans that draw extra power when starting. The calculator suggests a minimum inverter size with about 30 percent headroom. If you plan to run a fridge or water pump on backup, choose a larger inverter, as these draw a lot more when they start. A pure sine wave inverter is better for fans, fridges and electronics, and quieter in use.

Backup costs units too

A UPS does not create electricity. Every time the power returns, it recharges the batteries from the grid, and some energy is lost as heat in the process. Old batteries and cheap inverters waste more. A typical home UPS can add 20 to 50 units a month to your bill, which you can see with our electricity bill calculator. If your backup system is old and the bill keeps rising, it may be time to replace the batteries or move to a more efficient setup.

Batteries with solar

If you are adding solar, batteries let you use daytime solar energy during the evening or during outages. Size them for your actual backup needs rather than trying to store all your solar output, which would need a very large and expensive bank. Many families start with a hybrid inverter and a modest lithium battery, then add more later. Our solar calculator sizes the panels, and this battery backup calculator sizes the storage.

Practical tips for longer backup

  • Switch to DC inverter fans. They use around half the power of old fans, which nearly doubles backup time for the same battery.
  • Use LED lights only on backup. Remove old tube lights and energy savers from the UPS circuit.
  • Keep heavy loads off the UPS. Irons, heaters, kettles and microwaves drain batteries in minutes.
  • Keep batteries cool and ventilated. Heat shortens battery life, especially for lead-acid.
  • Check water levels monthly in tubular batteries and top up with distilled water only.

Common sizing mistakes

  • Buying a large inverter but a single small battery, so backup lasts minutes rather than hours.
  • Draining lead-acid batteries completely every day, which shortens their life sharply.
  • Mixing old and new batteries in the same bank, which drags the new ones down.
  • Putting the fridge on a small UPS without allowing for its starting surge.

Frequently asked questions

How long will a 200 Ah battery last?

It depends on the load. A single 12 V 200 Ah tubular battery runs about 260 watts, roughly two fans and some lights, for nearly four hours. Use the battery backup calculator above with your own appliances for a precise figure.

How many batteries do I need for 4 hours of backup?

Set your appliances in the calculator and increase the battery count until the backup time reaches four hours.

Is lithium worth the extra cost?

For daily use, usually yes. It delivers more usable energy per battery and lasts many more cycles. For occasional backup, tubular can still make sense.

Can I run an AC on a UPS?

Only with a large inverter and a big battery bank. For most homes, it is better to run an AC from solar during the day and keep the UPS for fans and lights.
